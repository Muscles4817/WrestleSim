# 13 — Releases and Firings

*Why people get cut, when it happens, how it happens, and what it does to everyone else.*

This answers the user question: **"When do they tend to fire?"**

---

## 1. The vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Release** | Contract terminated by the promotion before its term expires. The standard modern form. |
| **Not renewed / expired** | The contract simply ran out and wasn't extended. Quieter, less newsworthy, functionally the same. |
| **Future endeavoured** | Fan shorthand from the standard WWE press language ("we wish them well in their future endeavors"). |
| **Fired** | Usually reserved for termination for cause — a conduct breach. |
| **Asked for release** | The performer requested out. Granted or refused at the promotion's discretion. |
| **Furlough** | Temporary; used during the 2020 pandemic period. |
| **Sent to developmental** | A demotion that is often a precursor to release. |
| **Frozen out** | Not released, not used. The cruelest option and reasonably common. |

---

## 2. Why people get released — the reasons, ranked by frequency

### 2.1 Budget (the largest category, by a distance)
Not performance. **Cost.** A finance-driven headcount reduction that hits people who are
doing nothing wrong.

Characteristics:
- Comes in **waves**, not individually
- Clusters around **quarter boundaries**, **fiscal year-end**, **post-merger integration**,
  and **the aftermath of a large investment** (a big signing, a facility, an acquisition)
- Cuts the **middle and bottom** of the roster, which is where headcount is
- Frequently includes people the creative team wanted to use, because creative is not in
  the room
- Is announced in a single day, usually with no warning to the people involved

**The 2021 and 2023 WWE release waves** are the canonical modern examples — dozens of
performers in single-day announcements, explicitly framed as budget decisions, including
several with active storylines.

### 2.2 Creative has nothing for them
The most common individual reason. Not a judgment of ability — a judgment that the
promotion cannot find a use. Often true, often a self-fulfilling assessment made by people
who never tried.

### 2.3 The roster is too big
A structural version of 2.2. See [09-revenue-and-costs.md](09-revenue-and-costs.md) §2.2 —
when 160 people compete for 40 TV spots, 120 people are release candidates by arithmetic.

### 2.4 A regime change
A new head of creative or new ownership cuts the previous regime's signings. Extremely
common and extremely predictable. **A performer's most dangerous moment is when the person
who signed them leaves.**

### 2.5 Injury
Legally and reputationally sensitive, so it is rarely stated. In practice, a performer with
a long-term injury and no clear return date is a release candidate, and the tolling clause
means the promotion has already extended the contract. Promotions differ enormously in how
they handle this and it is one of the clearest indicators of an organisation's character.

### 2.6 Conduct
- Failed drug tests (WWE's wellness policy: escalating suspensions, 30/60 days, then
  termination)
- Legal issues, arrests
- Domestic violence allegations and other conduct that creates reputational or sponsor risk
- Backstage incidents, fights, insubordination
- Social media conduct

### 2.7 Reliability
No-shows, lateness, showing up impaired, refusing finishes. This is the oldest firing
reason in the business and remains the most defensible one.

### 2.8 Political loss
Being on the wrong side of someone with more standing. Real, unquantifiable, and
universally believed by performers to be more common than management admits.

### 2.9 Requested
The performer asked out. Reasons: creative frustration, schedule, family, a better offer,
health. Promotions sometimes refuse (the contract is enforceable) and sometimes grant
release as goodwill, and the choice is strategic — refusing keeps them off a rival's TV,
granting maintains a reputation for treating people decently.

## 3. When releases happen — the timing patterns

### 3.1 The recurring windows

| Window | Why |
|---|---|
| **Immediately after a major event** (post-WrestleMania, post-tentpole) | Storylines have concluded; nobody is mid-angle; the fiscal cycle resets |
| **End of a fiscal quarter** | Cost recognition |
| **After a corporate event** — merger, acquisition, sale, new ownership | Integration and synergy cuts. TKO's formation in 2023 produced exactly this. |
| **After a rights deal is signed** | The "look healthy" pressure is off; the roster built to impress a network is now just expensive |
| **When a large new cost lands** | A facility, a mass signing, an acquisition |
| **When a regime changes** | The new leadership's own signings replace the old |
| **Late spring and autumn** | Empirically the two heaviest seasons |
| **Anytime, for conduct** | Immediate |

### 3.2 The 90-day non-compete effect
WWE contracts typically carry a 90-day non-compete after release (often paid). This creates
a **predictable industry-wide calendar**: a release wave in April means an availability wave
in July, and every other promotion plans its summer around it.

The non-compete has a second effect: it cools the market. A performer who is white-hot with
sympathy on the day of release is a three-month-old story by the time they can work.

### 3.3 The "released while in a storyline" problem
Budget-driven cuts don't respect creative. Performers get released mid-feud, with a match
announced, sometimes on the day of a show. The promotion then has to write around it,
usually badly. This is a recurring, visible symptom of finance-led decision-making and is
one of the clearest tells that a promotion's creative department has no institutional power.

## 4. How a release happens (the process)

**At Tier 0, typically:**
1. A list is assembled by talent relations and finance, reviewed by leadership
2. Creative may or may not be consulted
3. Calls go out on a single day, often in a compressed window
4. A press statement is released, usually a standard formula
5. The performer's merchandise is pulled; their profile moves to an alumni section
6. The non-compete clock starts
7. The performer, in many cases, learns simultaneously with the public — several
   performers have reported finding out from social media

**At Tier 1–3:** a conversation, usually. Smaller promotions handle it more personally
because they have to see the person again.

**At Tier 4/5:** you simply stop being booked. There is no release; there is silence.

## 5. What releases do to the rest of the roster

Under-modelled and important:
- **Fear suppresses risk-taking.** A locker room that expects cuts stops pitching bold
  ideas.
- **It changes negotiating behaviour.** Performers with leverage start planning exits.
- **It damages trust in talent relations**, permanently, if handled badly.
- **It changes what performers optimise for** — from "get over" to "be visibly useful and
  uncontroversial", which produces a blander product.
- **It creates a class of the visibly safe** (the people who obviously won't be cut) and a
  class of the visibly precarious, and everyone knows which they are.
- **Conversely**, a promotion known for treating released talent well (paying out, giving
  notice, releasing people to opportunities) has a measurable recruiting advantage.

## 6. What releases do to the industry

**The release wave is the wrestling ecosystem's primary redistribution event.**

1. **Tier 1–3 promotions get an immediate talent injection** at a price they could never
   otherwise pay
2. **Audience interest spikes** for the released performers — sympathy plus curiosity plus
   the "unshackled" narrative
3. **Independent bookings surge** for 6–18 months
4. **Some performers become bigger than they were.** Freed from a system that had them in a
   losing role, with the audience's sympathy, and with a story ("they didn't see it, we
   do"), a released performer can be more valuable to a smaller promotion than they were to
   the large one. Cody Rhodes' 2016 departure and subsequent trajectory to co-founding AEW
   is the definitive modern example.
5. **The releasing promotion sometimes re-signs them later, at a higher price**, having
   watched someone else establish their value

### 6.1 The "released star" arc
A reliable pattern:
```
  Release ──► 90 days of sympathy and speculation ──► Debut somewhere else at peak heat
       ──► 3–9 months of strong reactions (novelty + goodwill)
       ──► Either: converted into a genuine new position
           Or: reverts to their prior level once the novelty burns off
```
The conversion rate is maybe 30–40%. The novelty of a new face is a real but temporary
asset; what happens in months 4–12 determines whether it was worth the money.

## 7. Firing decision-makers

Bookers, writers, and executives get removed too, and the pattern differs:
- **Bookers are changed after a sustained business decline**, typically 6–18 months after
  the decline is visible (organisations are slow)
- **The replacement is usually an internal veteran** or a returning previous booker — the
  industry recycles its leadership heavily
- **Writers churn constantly** at Tier 0, on 12–24 month cycles, which destroys
  institutional memory of characters and stories
- **Owners are almost never removed** — this is the structural problem in §1 of
  [02-promotion-anatomy.md](02-promotion-anatomy.md). The exceptions require external
  force: a board with real power, a sale, a scandal, or death.

## 8. Retirement and the exit ramp

Not all departures are releases. The ways a career ends:

| Exit | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Injury-forced** | The most common involuntary end. A neck, a back, or accumulated concussions. |
| **Gradual fade** | Fewer dates, lower card, then no dates. Most careers end this way, without an announcement. |
| **Planned retirement** | Rare, and the most valuable creatively — a retirement match is an event that can be built and sold |
| **Transition to a backstage role** | Producer, coach, talent relations. The best outcome for the industry, since it retains knowledge. |
| **Transition to another industry** | Acting, media, business, coaching, commentary |
| **The unretirement** | Very common. Wrestling retirements are famously non-binding, which devalues the retirement match as a device. |

**The retirement match** is one of the few remaining truly unrepeatable events in wrestling,
which makes it enormously valuable and means promotions should be far more careful with it
than they are.

## Sim implications

- **Release waves as scheduled/triggered events**, not per-person decisions, matches
  reality and creates the industry-wide redistribution dynamic.
- **Budget pressure as a force separate from creative judgment** is essential — the player
  being told to cut $2M of payroll regardless of who they want is the authentic experience.
- **The 90-day non-compete** creates a strategic calendar: released talent appears on the
  market in predictable cohorts.
- **The released-star arc** (§6.1) is a clean model: a temporary heat bonus on debut
  elsewhere, decaying over 3–9 months, with a conversion check.
- **Locker-room morale effects of releases** should be modelled — a wave should reduce
  risk-taking and increase departure requests among those with leverage.
- **Regime change causing signing purges** is a great AI-promotion behaviour that makes the
  world feel alive.
- **Retirement matches as a once-per-career, high-value, non-repeatable event** is worth
  implementing precisely because promotions in reality squander it.
