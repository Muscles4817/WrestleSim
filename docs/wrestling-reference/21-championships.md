# 21 — Championships

*What titles are for, how they gain and lose value, reign length, lineage, and the economics
of prestige.*

---

## 1. What a title actually is

A championship is a **portable, transferable marker of status** whose value is entirely a
function of what the audience believes about it. It has no intrinsic worth; a belt is a
prop. Its value is the accumulated weight of:

- **Who has held it** (lineage)
- **How hard it was to win**
- **How it is defended**
- **How the promotion treats it**
- **How long it has existed**

**This means title prestige is an asset that can be built, spent, and destroyed** — and it
is the promotion's most easily destroyed asset, because every booking decision touches it.

### 1.1 What a title does for the promotion
1. **Defines the top of the card.** Whoever holds the world title is, by definition, the
   main event.
2. **Creates automatic stakes** for any match involving it
3. **Generates a story engine** — the chase, the defence, the challenger
4. **Provides a measurable career achievement** the audience can track
5. **Is a merchandising and marketing asset**

---

## 2. The title hierarchy

| Tier | Function | Ideal count |
|---|---|---|
| **World / primary** | The top of the promotion. Defines the main event. | **One.** Two if a brand split demands it. |
| **Secondary / mid-card** | An elevation tool; a step toward the top | 1–2 |
| **Tag team** | Defines the tag division | 1 (2 with a brand split) |
| **Women's** | Defines the women's division | 1–2 |
| **Tertiary / specialty** (TV title, cruiserweight, hardcore, trios) | Gives a specific division identity and a reason to exist | 0–2 |

### 2.1 The title inflation problem
The single most common structural mistake. Symptoms:
- More titles than the television time can meaningfully feature
- Two "world" titles, neither of which is clearly the top
- Titles whose divisions have no depth
- Champions who don't defend

**Each additional title divides the prestige available.** A promotion with three world-level
titles has three mid-card titles. WWE's periods with multiple world championships, and the
various proliferations at Tier 1–2 promotions, demonstrate this reliably: the audience's
attention is finite, and a title it isn't paying attention to isn't a title.

**The rule:** a title is worth having only if the promotion can afford to give it enough
television to matter. If you can't feature a division for 10+ minutes a week, you can't
support its title.

---

## 3. How a title gains prestige

| Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|
| **A great champion holds it** | The title borrows their status |
| **It is defended frequently and credibly** | It looks like something worth having |
| **Big matches happen for it** | Association with the biggest moments |
| **Winning it visibly changes a career** | The audience sees it matter |
| **Long, dominant reigns** | Scarcity — if it changes rarely, holding it is significant |
| **Lineage** | "The 42nd champion" carries weight; history accumulates |
| **It is treated as important** by commentary, presentation, and the roster | Cheap and enormously effective |
| **It is fought for** — people want it visibly | Desire signals value |

## 4. How a title loses prestige

| Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Frequent, meaningless changes** | If it changes every month, holding it means nothing |
| **The champion loses non-title matches** | Devalues both champion and title |
| **Rare or absent defences** | An undefended title is a prop |
| **Being won by someone the audience rejects** | The audience's disbelief transfers to the belt |
| **Comedy** | A title used as a joke cannot be serious again quickly |
| **Being vacated** | Every vacancy weakens the lineage |
| **Being outshone** | If the world title match isn't the main event, it isn't the top title |
| **Too many titles** | Dilution |
| **Being ignored on television** | The fastest killer |

### 4.1 The non-title loss
Worth calling out because it is so common and so damaging. A champion losing a non-title
match is a booking convenience — it "protects" the title while giving someone a win. What it
actually communicates is that the champion is beatable and the title is a technicality. Used
sparingly it can set up a challenger; used routinely it hollows out the championship.

## 5. Reign length

### 5.1 The historical spread
| Era / promotion | Typical world title reign |
|---|---|
| **NWA territorial (1950s–1980s)** | 1–4 years. The travelling champion model. |
| **WWF 1980s** | 1–4 years (Hogan's first reign was ~4 years) |
| **Attitude era (1997–2001)** | Weeks to months. Frequent changes for TV moments. |
| **2000s–2010s WWE** | 2–8 months typical |
| **Modern WWE** | Highly variable; Roman Reigns' 2020–2024 reign (~1,300 days) is the modern outlier and was deliberately built as such |
| **NJPW** | 6–18 months typical; the champion is "the ace" |
| **Modern indies** | 6–18 months; scarcity is easier when you run 12 shows a year |

### 5.2 The trade-off
- **Long reigns** build prestige, create a definitive figure, and make the eventual title
  change an enormous event. Cost: the challenger pool gets exhausted, and if the champion
  isn't compelling the whole top of the card stagnates.
- **Short reigns** create frequent big moments and let more people be elevated. Cost: the
  title becomes a prop that circulates, and no individual reign means anything.

**The evidence favours longer.** Almost every title reign remembered as significant was long.
Almost no one remembers a two-week reign except as trivia. The frequent-change approach
produces short-term TV moments at the cost of the asset that generates all future moments.

### 5.3 The transitional champion
Someone given the title briefly to move it from A to C without A losing to C directly.
Legitimate tool, but each use costs lineage value, and a transitional reign that goes wrong
(injury, a plan change) leaves the belt on someone who was never meant to have it.

## 6. The title change

### 6.1 When to change a title
- **At a big show.** A title changing on free television is a statement that free television
  matters — powerful once or twice a year, corrosive as a habit.
- **At the end of a chase**, as the payoff to a long story
- **To elevate someone at their peak heat**, not after it
- **When the current champion has run out of credible challengers**
- **When a story demands it**

### 6.2 When not to change a title
- To create a surprise for its own sake
- Because the champion is injured (vacating and a tournament is usually cleaner)
- To reward someone for loyalty
- Because the audience is bored — boredom is a booking problem, not a title problem
- Right before the champion leaves the promotion (this happens and it's always a mess)

### 6.3 The mechanics of a good title win
- The winner has been built (see [12](12-pushes-and-positioning.md) §3.1)
- The win is clean, or at least decisive
- The moment is given time — the celebration, the reaction, the image
- **The following week matters**: the new champion must immediately look like a champion

## 7. Lineage and history

The accumulated history of a title is a genuine asset:
- The **NWA World Heavyweight Championship** lineage (from 1948) is the most storied in
  American wrestling and was, for decades, the single most prestigious prize in the business
  because it was defended across every territory against every territory's top star
- **The IWGP Heavyweight Championship** (1987–2021, then unified into the IWGP World
  Heavyweight Championship) carries similar weight in Japan
- **CMLL's titles** date to the 1930s–40s and are treated with formality

Modern promotions periodically **retire and replace** titles for design or branding reasons.
This trades accumulated lineage for a fresh start and is almost always a net loss unless the
old title had been genuinely devalued.

---

## 8. Special title structures

### 8.1 The travelling champion (NWA model)
The champion works every territory, defends against each promotion's local hero, and
usually retains via a finish that protects the local star (count-out, DQ, time limit
draw). The genius of this: **the local hero gets a "he almost did it" moment**, the
champion stays champion, and the local promotion sells out on the rematch.

Ric Flair's NWA reigns are the definitive execution — ~300 dates a year, working every
territory, making every local star look like a world beater.

### 8.2 The briefcase / contract (Money in the Bank)
A guaranteed future title shot, cashable at any time. Brilliant device:
- Creates a year-long story from a single match
- Every champion is permanently vulnerable
- The cash-in is a self-contained moment that can be deployed at maximum impact

**The failure mode:** predictability once the audience learns the pattern, and devaluation
if the cash-in fails too often.

### 8.3 The apuesta (mask/hair)
Not a title but functions as one — a permanent, irreversible stake. See
[25-international-models.md](25-international-models.md).

### 8.4 The unification
Merging two titles. Resolves inflation, creates a big match, and permanently deletes one
lineage. Usually correct when a promotion has over-proliferated, and always a one-way door.

---

## 9. The champion's booking

A champion should:
- **Defend regularly** — monthly at minimum on a weekly-TV schedule
- **Win most matches** — an unbeaten champion is fine; a champion losing half their matches
  is not a champion
- **Be present** — an absent champion leaves the top of the card empty
- **Elevate challengers** — every credible challenger they make is a future main eventer
- **Look like the best** — commentary, presentation, and booking should agree

**The most valuable thing a long-reigning champion does is create challengers.** A two-year
reign that produces four new main-event-level performers is worth vastly more than one that
beats the same three people repeatedly.

## Sim implications

- **Titles are modelled in WrestleSim** as of the championships work: `Title`, `TitleReign`
  and `TitleRegistry` in `WrestlingSim.Core/Models/World`, with the prestige rules in
  `WrestlingSim.Core/Engine/TitleEconomy.cs`. Everything below describes what that
  implements.
- **Title prestige as a first-class value** that rises and falls per §3 and §4 is the core
  mechanic. It should modify: the crowd's baseline investment in any match for it, the
  status transfer from winning it, and the promotion's overall drawing power.
- **Reign length effects**: prestige should accumulate with reign length and with each
  credible defence, and the drop when the title changes should scale with the outgoing
  champion's accumulated prestige — making a long reign's ending a genuinely large event.
- **Title inflation**: total prestige available should be roughly fixed and divided among
  titles, so adding a fourth belt measurably weakens the other three. This makes "should we
  add a title?" a real decision with a real cost.
- **The non-title loss** as a specific, small, cumulative prestige penalty is a nice detail
  that punishes a real and common booking shortcut.
- **The Money in the Bank device** — a deferred, cashable title shot — is an excellent
  discrete mechanic that creates a long-running strategic decision for the player.
- **The travelling champion model** would be a distinctive alternative structure for a
  territory-era game mode, with the local-hero-protection finish as its signature move.
