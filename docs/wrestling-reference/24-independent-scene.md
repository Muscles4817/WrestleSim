# 24 — The Independent Scene

*The ecosystem underneath the majors: how it works, how it pays, and why it matters
disproportionately to the whole industry.*

---

## 1. What "independent" means

An independent promotion is one that:
- Has no national television deal providing guaranteed revenue
- Books talent **per date** rather than exclusively
- Operates show-to-show financially

The term covers everything from a 3,000-capacity super-indie with an international
reputation to eight guys in a VFW hall.

---

## 2. Why the indies matter more than their revenue suggests

1. **They are the industry's training system.** Almost every performer in every promotion
   learned their craft on indie shows in front of small crowds who owed them nothing.
2. **They are the R&D department.** New styles, new gimmicks, and new match concepts appear
   on the indies years before the majors adopt them.
3. **They are the talent market.** The majors scout here; every signing wave is an indie
   depopulation.
4. **They absorb the majors' releases**, which keeps performers working and keeps their
   value alive.
5. **They preserve regional wrestling culture** that the majors have abandoned.
6. **They set the ceiling on what majors can get away with**, because a good alternative
   exists.

---

## 3. The tiers within the indies

### 3.1 The super-indie (Tier 3)
Examples across eras: ECW (pre-national), Ring of Honor (2002–2018), PWG, Chikara, Evolve,
Progress, RevPro, wXw, Beyond, GCW, DEFY.

- 12–40 shows a year
- 500–2,500 capacity, sometimes larger for a marquee event
- Streaming/VOD platform, sometimes a niche TV deal
- Pays $300–2,000 per date for names
- **Internationally recognised roster**, largely shared with other super-indies
- Reputation is the product: a PWG show sold out in minutes because of what the brand meant

**The strategic position:** super-indies are simultaneously the most creatively free
promotions in wrestling and the most structurally fragile, because they cannot retain the
talent they make valuable.

### 3.2 The regional independent (Tier 4)
- 6–24 shows a year, 2–4 towns
- 150–800 capacity
- Local roster plus one or two travelling names per show
- $50–300 per date
- Often attached to a wrestling school
- Profit measured in hundreds of dollars per show

### 3.3 The local (Tier 5)
- Under 12 shows a year, often fewer
- Under 200 people
- Frequently a school showcase or a charity/fair booking
- Performers often unpaid

## 4. The economics of an independent career

### 4.1 The costs of being an independent wrestler
| Cost | Typical |
|---|---|
| Training | $1,500–5,000 upfront, plus monthly |
| Gear | $300–1,500 per set; boots $200–500; replaced regularly |
| Travel | The dominant cost. Fuel, flights, hotels, splitting rooms 4-to-a-room |
| Merchandise inventory | $500–3,000 upfront to have a table |
| Photos/media | Headshots, video, a website |
| Medical | Out of pocket, entirely |
| Time | Most work day jobs; wrestling schedules around them |

### 4.2 The revenue
| Source | Typical |
|---|---|
| Booking fee | $50–500 per date; $500–2,000 for a recognised name |
| Merch table | $100–1,000 per date for someone with a following — **often exceeding the booking fee** |
| Seminars/coaching | $200–1,000 per session for established names |
| International tours | The best money on the indies; a UK/Japan/Mexico tour can pay a month's income |
| Streaming/Patreon/Cameo | Growing; some performers earn more from this than from wrestling |

### 4.3 The realistic picture
A busy, well-regarded independent wrestler working 60–100 dates a year might gross
$40,000–$120,000 before travel costs, with no benefits, no security, and full injury risk.
**The great majority of independent wrestlers earn under $15,000 a year and hold other
jobs.**

## 5. How independent promotions book

### 5.1 The per-date model
No contracts. The promoter books each show individually, negotiating with each performer
(or their booker/agent). Consequences:
- **Nobody is exclusive.** The same wrestler works for six promotions in a month.
- **Storylines are hard** — you cannot guarantee availability
- **Dream matches are easy** — no exclusivity barrier
- **Cross-promotion is normal** and is a major creative asset
- **A talent you build can be booked as a star by your competitor next week**

### 5.2 What indies book for
- **The match, not the story.** Without weekly TV, sustained storytelling is difficult, so
  indies sell match-ups and cards rather than arcs. The exception is the promotions that run
  frequently enough to build (a monthly show can sustain a slow story).
- **The event.** Tournaments, anniversary shows, marquee weekends.
- **The reputation.** A promotion known for delivering great matches sells on that alone.

### 5.3 The tournament tradition
Indies use tournaments heavily because they solve the structural problem: a one-night
tournament creates stakes, a bracket, drama, and a champion with no continuity required.
The Battle of Los Angeles, the Super 8, King of Trios, and dozens of others built promotion
identities out of a single annual weekend.

## 6. The indie boom-and-bust cycle

A repeating pattern, roughly every 8–12 years:

```
  1. A generation of talent matures on the indies simultaneously
  2. Word of mouth builds; tape/streaming spreads the reputation
  3. Attendance and prices rise; new promotions form; the scene is "hot"
  4. The majors notice and sign the top 20–40 performers over 2–3 years
  5. The scene is hollowed out; the promotions that built those stars get nothing
  6. Attendance falls; several promotions fold
  7. A new generation begins developing in the gap
  8. Return to 1
```

**Documented iterations:** the 1990s ECW/indie scene → WWF/WCW signings 1996–99; the
2003–2008 ROH generation → WWE/TNA; the 2014–2019 boom → AEW's formation and WWE's
counter-signing; the 2022–2025 rebuild.

**The structural injustice:** promotions that develop talent capture almost none of the
value they create. There is no transfer fee, no development compensation, no equity. A
promotion that spends three years making someone a star loses them for nothing.

---

## 7. Cross-promotion and the shared universe

Because nobody is exclusive, the independent scene functions as a **shared universe**:
- A champion of one promotion can defend in another
- Rivalries carry across promotions
- Invasion angles between indies are possible and real
- A performer's "record" is understood across the whole scene by fans who follow it

This is genuinely unique in wrestling and produces things the majors cannot: true dream
matches, unpredictable card composition, and a sense that anything can happen.

**Its weakness:** no promotion owns anything. A story built across four promotions belongs
to none of them and cannot be monetised by any of them beyond a single show.

---

## 8. WrestleMania weekend

The independent scene's single most important institution. See
[10-talent-acquisition.md](10-talent-acquisition.md) §5.4. In summary:
- 30–60 shows over four days in the host city
- Every major indie runs its biggest show of the year
- The biggest indie paydays of the year
- A de facto convention, scouting event, and industry gathering
- **A single weekend that can represent 20–40% of a super-indie's annual revenue**

## 9. The failure modes of an independent promotion

| Failure | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Overexpansion after a hot year** | Fixed costs added on variable revenue |
| **Losing the anchor talent** | The 2–3 people the audience came for get signed |
| **A bad venue change** | The room is the product; moving to a bigger, colder building kills the atmosphere |
| **Running too often** | Saturating a small audience |
| **Not paying people** | Word travels instantly; the talent pool closes |
| **Promoter burnout** | The most common cause of quiet deaths — it's a second job that consumes everything |
| **A single bad show** | With a small devoted audience, reputation is fragile |
| **Insurance or venue loss** | An administrative problem that ends a promotion |
| **Competing with a major's tour date** | Nobody buys two tickets in one week |

---

## 10. What indies do better than majors

Worth stating because it explains why they persist:
- **Atmosphere.** A packed 600-seat room is louder and more intense than a half-full arena.
- **Freedom.** No standards department, no network notes, no brand safety.
- **Access.** Fans meet performers. That relationship is the deepest kind of fandom.
- **Match quality.** Performers with no scripted constraints and something to prove.
- **Risk.** New ideas can be tried; failure costs one night, not a quarter.
- **Community.** Regulars know each other; the show is a social institution.

---

## Sim implications

- **A living independent scene** underneath the player's promotion — with its own
  promotions, its own developing talent, its own boom-bust cycle — is what makes a wrestling
  world feel real rather than like a static roster list.
- **Per-date booking** as a distinct contract mode: cheap, flexible, non-exclusive, and
  with the permanent risk that a rival books the same person.
- **The talent-development-without-capture problem** is a great strategic tension for a
  player running an indie: building a star raises your business and guarantees you lose them.
- **The boom-bust cycle** as a world-level rhythm gives long games an authentic shape and
  explains why the talent pool waxes and wanes.
- **WrestleMania-weekend-style convergence events** — a fixed annual weekend where every
  promotion runs and every scout attends — is a superb recurring event.
- **Venue atmosphere as a multiplier** independent of capacity: a packed small room should
  out-perform a half-empty large one on crowd reaction while under-performing on revenue.
