# 26 — Eras and History

*The periods of professional wrestling, what changed at each boundary, and what each era
teaches about how the business works.*

---

## 1. Why eras matter for modelling

Almost every "rule" in wrestling is era-dependent. Contracts, schedules, revenue mixes,
match styles, audience expectations, and content standards all changed at identifiable
boundaries. Any claim about "how wrestling works" needs an era attached.

The boundaries below are the ones where **the underlying economics changed**, not merely
the fashion.

---

## 2. The eras

### 2.1 Carnival and legitimate contests (1860s–1920s)
- Wrestling as a genuine competitive sport at fairs and athletic clubs
- Catch-as-catch-can, Greco-Roman, and regional folk styles
- Matches were long, often dull, and sometimes genuinely competitive
- **The transition to worked matches** happened gradually because real contests were bad
  entertainment: they could last hours with nothing happening
- The **carnival "athletic show"** — where a wrestler took on all comers — is the origin of
  much of the industry's vocabulary and its culture of secrecy

**What it teaches:** wrestling became worked because worked was *better entertainment*, not
because of fraud. The audience wanted a story, and the business gave them one.

### 2.2 The Gold Dust Trio and the birth of modern wrestling (1920s–1930s)
- Ed "Strangler" Lewis, Billy Sandow, and Toots Mondt develop the modern format: shorter
  matches, high spots, storylines, feuds, and a touring troupe
- The invention of **"Slam Bang Western Style Wrestling"** — essentially the template for
  everything since

**What it teaches:** the modern product was deliberately designed, in one place, by people
solving an entertainment problem.

### 2.3 The National Wrestling Alliance and the territory system (1948–1983)
- The **NWA** forms as a cartel of regional promoters
- Each promoter has an exclusive territory; talent circulates between them
- A single **NWA World Champion** tours all territories, defending against each region's
  local hero
- Television is local, cheap, and functions as **an advertisement for the house shows**
- Kayfabe is absolute

**Structural properties:**
- Talent moved every 6–18 months to stay fresh — the **built-in freshness mechanism**
- Local heroes could be genuine regional superstars without national fame
- The system produced enormous numbers of skilled workers because everyone worked 5–6
  nights a week
- Promoters colluded on talent (blacklisting was real and effective)

**What it teaches:** wrestling's most productive talent-development system was a byproduct
of geographic fragmentation. The volume of work and the constant movement between markets
did what no training facility can.

### 2.4 The national expansion (1984–1992)
- Vince McMahon Jr. takes the WWF national via cable and syndication, raiding territories
  for their stars
- **WrestleMania** (1985) invents the wrestling supercard as a mainstream media event
- Hulk Hogan and the "Rock 'n' Wrestling Connection" bring wrestling into pop culture
- Territories die, one by one, unable to compete with national exposure
- Pay-per-view emerges as a revenue line
- **Merchandising and licensing** become major, driven by a children's audience

**Simultaneously:** Jim Crockett Promotions consolidates the NWA's Southern territories,
overextends, and sells to Ted Turner in 1988, becoming WCW.

**What it teaches:** national distribution beats regional quality. The territories weren't
worse — they were smaller, and scale won.

**The dark side:** the steroid era, the schedule at its most brutal, and the beginning of
the mortality crisis among performers.

### 2.5 The New Generation and the lean years (1993–1996)
- Post-steroid-trial WWF; smaller performers pushed (Bret Hart, Shawn Michaels)
- Business declines significantly; house shows shrink
- **Raw** debuts (1993) — the weekly live episodic format is invented
- ECW forms and develops a genuinely alternative product
- WCW under Eric Bischoff begins signing WWF talent

**What it teaches:** technical excellence (this era had exceptional in-ring work) does not by
itself draw. The business was creatively strong and commercially weak simultaneously.

### 2.6 The Monday Night Wars and the Attitude Era (1996–2001)
The industry's commercial peak.

- **Nitro vs Raw**, head to head, Monday nights
- The **nWo** angle (1996) — the highest-drawing storyline in American wrestling history
- WCW leads for 83 consecutive weeks (1996–1998)
- WWF responds with the **Attitude Era**: edgier content, Austin vs McMahon, The Rock,
  D-Generation X, reality-blurring storylines
- **Peak numbers:** combined weekly audiences of 9–11 million; Raw peaks around 8 million
- Talent wages reach their historical maximum, driven by two bidders
- WCW collapses through 1999–2000 and is sold to WWF in **March 2001**
- ECW folds the same year

**What it teaches:**
1. **Competition is the industry's growth engine.** Two well-funded promotions produced the
   biggest audience wrestling has ever had.
2. **Reality-adjacent storytelling** is the most potent creative tool available.
3. **Escalation is a treadmill.** The crash-TV format consumed ideas at an unsustainable
   rate and both companies ran out.
4. **A promotion can die from creative-control clauses and cost inflation** while its
   parent company is enormously profitable.

### 2.7 The monopoly and the Ruthless Aggression era (2001–2007)
- WWE owns the field; WCW and ECW libraries acquired
- The **Invasion** angle (2001) squanders the biggest premise in wrestling history by
  booking the acquired talent as inferior
- The **first brand split** (2002) to manage an oversized roster
- Wages compress; talent leverage collapses
- Strong in-ring era (Angle, Benoit, Guerrero, Lesnar, Mysterio, Edge)
- **2005–2007:** Eddie Guerrero's death, the wellness policy, then the Benoit tragedy —
  the industry's reckoning with its health record

**What it teaches:** monopoly is bad for talent and, eventually, bad for the product,
because the pressure to be excellent disappears.

### 2.8 The PG era (2008–2013)
- Content rating shifts to PG, driven by advertisers, licensing (Mattel), and public
  company considerations
- John Cena as the franchise; a children- and family-focused product
- TNA attempts a national challenge, peaks around 2010, and fails
- **Ring of Honor and the independent scene** become a genuine alternative for hardcore fans
- CM Punk's 2011 "pipe bomb" briefly demonstrates the power of reality-based storytelling
  in the modern context

**What it teaches:** content restriction is a business decision with real creative costs and
real financial benefits, and reasonable people weigh them differently.

### 2.9 The Network and Reality eras (2014–2018)
- **WWE Network** (2014) — the PPV model ends; direct-to-consumer subscription begins
- The **Yes Movement** (2013–14): an audience-led revolt that management eventually
  accommodated, producing one of the era's best-received moments
- **NXT** develops into a genuinely acclaimed brand under Triple H
- **The Women's Evolution** (2015–) — a structural, permanent expansion of the women's
  division, moving from short matches to main events and their own tentpole matches
- The **second brand split** (2016)
- The independent scene enters a boom period

**What it teaches:** the direct-to-consumer transition permanently changed what an event
is for, and it removed the industry's clearest measurement of drawing power.

### 2.10 The rights-fee era and renewed competition (2018–present)
- **2018:** the Raw/SmackDown rights deals (~$470M/yr combined) reset the industry's
  economics — rights fees now dwarf every other line
- **2019: AEW forms**, backed by Shahid Khan, with a TNT deal. Genuine national competition
  returns for the first time since 2001.
- **2020–2021:** the pandemic — empty-arena shows, the ThunderDome, mass releases,
  the Performance Center period. A natural experiment demonstrating exactly how much a crowd
  contributes to the product (answer: enormously).
- **2021:** WWE Network content moves to Peacock
- **2022:** Vince McMahon's departure amid investigations; Paul Levesque assumes creative
- **2023:** WWE and UFC merge into **TKO Group Holdings** under Endeavor
- **2024–2025:** Raw moves to **Netflix** (~$5B/10yr, reported); WWE acquires AAA; the
  streaming era proper
- Talent wages rise again with two bidders

**What it teaches:** the divergence of audience size and revenue is now the defining fact.
Wrestling is more valuable than it has ever been while being watched by a fraction of its
1999 audience.

## 3. Cross-era patterns

### 3.1 The boom cycle
Wrestling has had roughly three genuine mainstream booms: **1984–1990** (Hogan), **1997–2000**
(Attitude), and arguably a smaller one **2014–2016**. Each had the same ingredients:
1. A performer who transcends wrestling and reaches non-fans
2. A cultural moment the product connects to
3. Competition or a sense of stakes
4. A distribution change that reaches new people

**Booms last 3–6 years and are followed by a decline of similar length.** The decline is not
caused by bad booking; it is caused by the boom's audience aging out of the behaviour, the
formula becoming familiar, and the star at the centre getting older.

### 3.2 The competition correlation
Every period of significant growth coincided with competition:
- 1984–88: WWF vs the territories vs Crockett
- 1996–2001: WWF vs WCW vs ECW
- 2019–: WWE vs AEW

Every period of stagnation coincided with monopoly. **This is the strongest empirical
pattern in wrestling history.**

### 3.3 What never changes
- Face in peril
- Betrayal as the strongest angle
- The audience deciding who they like regardless of booking
- Freshness decay
- The tension between drawing and quality
- Owners staying too long

## 4. The era boundaries as design parameters

For modelling purposes, the variables that actually differ by era:

| Variable | 1975 | 1988 | 1999 | 2010 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue mix | Gate 80% | Gate 55%, PPV 20% | PPV 35%, TV 25% | TV 40%, PPV 20% | Media 62% |
| Shows/year (major) | 300+ | 350+ | 380+ | 300 | 270 |
| Dates per performer | 250+ | 250+ | 220 | 180 | 120 |
| Contract type | Per-date, gate % | Guarantees emerging | Large guarantees | Modest guarantees | Large guarantees |
| Content rating | Family-ish | Family | TV-14+ | PG | PG-14 mixed |
| Match length (main) | 20–60 | 12–20 | 10–20 | 15–25 | 15–30 |
| Kayfabe | Absolute | Eroding | Abandoned | Ironic | Meta |
| Women's division | Minimal | Minimal | Exploitative | Developing | Full |
| Medical policy | None | None | Minimal | Wellness policy | Comprehensive |
| Title reign length | 1–4 years | 6mo–4yr | Weeks–months | 2–8 months | Variable |
| Star creation rate | High | High | High | Low | Low–medium |

## Sim implications

- **Era as a global game parameter set** using the table in §4 gives every historical mode a
  genuinely different feel with a single configuration object rather than special-case code.
- **The boom cycle** as a world-level rhythm — 3–6 years up, 3–6 years down, triggered by a
  transcendent star plus a distribution change — would make long campaigns feel historical
  rather than linear.
- **The competition correlation** should be mechanical: the existence of a credible rival
  should raise wages, raise audience interest overall, and raise the rate of star creation.
  Killing your rival should feel like a victory that quietly costs you.
- **Era transitions as events** the player lives through (the arrival of PPV, the launch of a
  streaming service, a content-rating change forced by a sponsor) turn history into gameplay.
- **The territory-era freshness mechanism** — talent rotating between promotions every
  6–18 months — is worth implementing as a distinct early-era rule, because it explains why
  that era produced so many complete performers.
