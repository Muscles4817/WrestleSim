# 10 — Talent Acquisition

*Who decides who gets hired, how they decide, where talent comes from, and when hiring
actually happens.*

This answers the user questions: **"Who decides who gets hired? How do they decide? When do
they tend to hire?"**

---

## 1. Who decides

The decision structure varies by tier, and knowing which structure you're in explains
almost every hiring outcome.

### Tier 0 (WWE-scale)
```
   OWNER / TOP EXECUTIVE          ← final say on anyone notable; can veto or impose
            ▲
   HEAD OF TALENT RELATIONS       ← runs the process, negotiates, presents candidates
            ▲
   HEAD OF RECRUITING / SCOUTING  ← sources candidates, runs tryouts
            ▲
   SCOUTS + COACHES               ← evaluate, recommend
            ▲
   TRYOUT / COMBINE               ← the funnel
```
- **Routine developmental signings** are made by recruiting and talent relations.
- **Any signing with a name** (an established indie star, a former rival's talent, an
  athlete from another sport) goes to the top. The owner or the head of creative wants to
  see them, meet them, or watch tape.
- **Historically, one person's taste dominated.** For four decades, if Vince McMahon liked
  someone's look, they were hired; if he didn't, no amount of ability mattered. This is not
  an exaggeration and it is the single largest explanatory factor in WWE hiring from
  1984–2022.
- Post-2022 the structure is more conventionally corporate: a recruiting department, a
  head of creative (Paul Levesque) with talent influence, and TKO-level cost oversight.

### Tier 1 (AEW-scale)
- **The owner decides, often directly and personally.** Tony Khan negotiates with talent
  himself, which is unusual at this scale and produces both the promotion's speed and its
  roster-bloat problem.
- Roster input from veteran locker-room leaders (an EVP structure existed at AEW's founding)
- **Far less formal scouting.** Signings come from watching the independent scene and from
  who becomes available.

### Tier 2/3
- **The booker/owner decides**, based on: who they've seen, who other bookers recommend,
  who is available, and who the audience asked for.
- Almost no formal scouting. The network is the scouting system.

### Tier 4/5
- **The promoter decides**, usually from: the local school's graduates, people who have
  worked for them before, and whoever a trusted worker vouches for.
- **Vouching is the entire system.** "So-and-so is good, shows up on time, doesn't hurt
  people" from a trusted worker is worth more than any tape.

## 2. Where talent comes from — the sources

### 2.1 The independent scene (the primary pipeline)
Most performers at every level came up on the indies: trained at a school, worked Tier 5
and 4 shows for years, built a reputation, got booked at Tier 3, got noticed.

**What this produces:** performers who can work a crowd, improvise, handle a bad night, and
have a character, because they had to develop one in front of 80 people who didn't know
them. This is the industry's most reliable source of complete performers.

### 2.2 Athletic conversion (the "great athlete" pipeline)
Recruiting from other sports — American football (the single largest source), amateur
wrestling, rugby, powerlifting, bodybuilding, MMA, gymnastics, track.

**What this produces:** exceptional physical specimens with no performance instinct.
Success rate is low but the hits are enormous (Lesnar, Goldberg, Angle, Ronda Rousey,
Bobby Lashley, Roman Reigns and a very long list of others came from other sports).

**The trade-off, honestly stated:** the athletic pipeline produces the highest ceiling and
the lowest hit rate. Look and athleticism are visible at a tryout; connection is not, and
connection is what actually pays.

### 2.3 Legacy / family
Wrestling is unusually dynastic (Harts, Von Erichs, Anoaʻi/Samoan family, Guerreros,
Rhodes, Flairs, Windhams, Briscoes, Tsuruta-lineage dojos). Reasons:
- Access to training from childhood
- Understanding of the business's unwritten rules
- Instant name recognition with the audience
- Networks and vouching

**Advantage is real and so is the resentment.** A legacy performer who isn't good enough
gets more chances than a non-legacy one, and the locker room notices.

### 2.4 International recruitment
Signing from Japan, Mexico, the UK, Europe, Australia, India. Reasons: distinct styles,
access to those markets, and (increasingly) global streaming making international audiences
directly monetisable.

### 2.5 Celebrity / crossover
Actors, musicians, athletes brought in for a match or an angle. **Not talent acquisition
in the normal sense** — it is marketing spend. Successful when the celebrity takes it
seriously and puts a real star over (Lawrence Taylor, Mike Tyson, Bad Bunny, Logan Paul);
disastrous when the celebrity is protected at the roster's expense.

### 2.6 Poaching from rivals
Signing a competitor's talent. Motives:
1. **Acquisition** — you want them
2. **Denial** — you don't want your rival to have them
3. **Signal** — a public statement that you're winning

Historically the escalation mechanism of every wrestling war. The 1996 nWo formed out of
WCW signing WWF talent; the 2019–2021 period saw large-scale movement in both directions.
**Poached talent underperforms more often than not**, because the character that made them
valuable was built in a context that doesn't travel.

### 2.7 The re-sign / the return
Bringing back someone previously released or departed. Extremely common, quite often
successful — because the audience's affection for a returning name is a **free asset** you
don't have to build. See [17-heat-and-getting-over.md](17-heat-and-getting-over.md) on
absence as a heat generator.

## 3. How they decide — the evaluation criteria

### 3.1 What is actually assessed, in the order it is actually weighted

| Rank | Criterion | Why it dominates |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Look / presence** | Instantly evaluable, correlates with how the camera reads them, and decision-makers trust their own eye |
| 2 | **Athleticism / physical ability** | Measurable at a tryout; predicts durability and match ceiling |
| 3 | **Charisma / "it"** | The thing that actually pays, but the hardest to assess in a gym |
| 4 | **Promo ability** | Tested at tryouts, weighted more heavily since ~2015 |
| 5 | **Existing name value** | For established indie talent — how much audience comes with them |
| 6 | **Trainability / attitude** | Coaches' assessment; heavily weighted for young signings |
| 7 | **In-ring skill** | Weighted much less than fans expect, because it is teachable |
| 8 | **Reputation** (safe, professional, drug-free, no red flags) | A veto criterion rather than a positive one |
| 9 | **Age** | Under 25 is preferred at Tier 0 developmental; over 35 needs a name |
| 10 | **Fit with a gap** | "We need a big heel" / "we have no cruiserweights" |

### 3.2 The three questions a decision-maker is really asking
1. **"Can I put this person on television tomorrow and have the audience believe they
   belong?"** — this is the look question, and it is why look dominates
2. **"Can I sell a t-shirt with this person on it?"** — the character question
3. **"Will this person be a problem?"** — the reliability question

### 3.3 What a tryout looks like (Tier 0 model)
A multi-day combine, typically 3–5 days, 20–60 participants:
- **Day 1:** athletic testing, conditioning, basic bumps. Attrition begins immediately —
  conditioning washes people out on day one.
- **Days 2–3:** wrestling fundamentals, ring psychology basics, drills
- **Promo work:** a camera, a prompt, 60–90 seconds. This eliminates a lot of athletes.
- **Character exercise:** who are you, what's your hook
- **Observation of attitude:** who helps others, who complains, who quits
- **Interviews:** background, motivation, availability, red flags

Coaches submit assessments; recruiting makes recommendations; contracts are offered to a
small fraction — often 10–25% of attendees, sometimes zero.

### 3.4 What gets someone signed who "shouldn't" be
- A decision-maker personally likes them
- They look like someone who was previously successful (pattern-matching is rampant)
- A famous relative
- Denial value (a rival wanted them)
- A market they unlock
- Media/social following that management believes converts

### 3.5 What gets someone passed over who "should" be signed
- Too small (the single most common answer, historically, and repeatedly proven wrong by
  the audience — Bryan, Rey Mysterio, Eddie Guerrero, Mick Foley)
- Doesn't look like a wrestler
- Bad tryout day (a genuinely unfair filter — one bad promo in a gym is not predictive)
- Reputation issue, real or rumoured
- No clear "spot" for them on the current roster
- Already 30+ without a name

## 4. Developmental systems

### 4.1 The WWE Performance Center model (2013–)
- A purpose-built facility (Orlando) with rings, gym, promo studio, media training,
  physiotherapy, film review
- Full-time contracts with a modest salary; performers train daily
- **NXT** as the televised proving ground
- Typical developmental period: **12–36 months** before a call-up

**Strengths:** consistent fundamentals, safety, conditioning, media polish, injury
prevention, a controlled environment for athletic converts.
**Weaknesses:** homogenisation. Everyone learns the same offence, the same structure, the
same promo cadence. The 2018–2021 cohort was widely criticised as technically proficient
and interchangeable. **Character is not efficiently manufacturable in a classroom** — it
comes from repetition in front of strangers who owe you nothing.

### 4.2 The territory model (historical)
A young worker started in a territory, worked 5–6 nights a week doing jobs, learned by
being in the ring with veterans, and moved territories every 6–18 months to stay fresh.
**Enormously effective and enormously brutal.** It produced complete performers because it
gave them thousands of matches in front of real crowds.

### 4.3 The Japanese dojo / Young Lion system
- Live-in dojo, extreme physical conditioning, strict hierarchy
- Young Lions wear plain black trunks, do the openers, and lose — often for **years**
- **The excursion:** a promising young wrestler is sent abroad (Mexico, the US, Europe) for
  1–3 years, returns with a new name, new look, new character, and is immediately elevated
- The system is slow, harsh, and produces a very high ratio of complete performers

**The excursion is the single most elegant star-making device in wrestling.** It solves the
"we've seen this kid lose for three years" problem by physically removing them and
returning them as a different, credible person.

### 4.4 Independent training schools
Where most of the world's wrestlers actually learn. Quality varies from world-class
(the historic Hart Dungeon, Killer Kowalski's, Lance Storm's, the Nightmare Factory,
various regional schools) to predatory (schools that take tuition and teach nothing).

**Economics:** tuition of $1,500–5,000, plus monthly fees; the school runs shows where
students work for free or for gas money. It is, at its best, an apprenticeship, and at its
worst, a business selling a dream.

## 5. When they hire — the timing

### 5.1 The recurring hiring windows

| Window | Trigger | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **After a talent raid / roster loss** | A rival signs your people | Reactive, fast, sometimes panicky |
| **Before a TV rights negotiation** | 12–18 months out | Aggressive signing to look healthy and deep |
| **After a competitor's release wave** | A rival cuts 20 people | Immediate opportunistic pickup — the best value in the business |
| **Contract expiry season** | Rivals' deals lapsing | Negotiations start months before the date; "free agency" clusters |
| **Post-tentpole** | After WrestleMania | Roster refresh; call-ups from developmental |
| **Annual tryout cycle** | Scheduled combines, 2–4 per year | Steady-state developmental intake |
| **When a specific gap opens** | An injury, a retirement, a division that has no depth | Targeted |
| **When someone becomes available who was never expected to be** | A major star leaves a rival | Opportunistic and usually expensive |

### 5.2 The single most predictable hiring pattern
**A major promotion's release wave is followed, within 90 days, by every other promotion in
the industry making offers.** The 90-day non-compete common in WWE contracts is precisely
the mechanism that makes this a wave rather than a trickle — a cohort is released
together, all become available the same week, and the entire industry restructures around
it. Tier 1–3 promotions plan their creative around these dates.

### 5.3 Seasonality
- Developmental call-ups cluster **after the biggest show of the year** (post-Mania) and
  **in the autumn** (a second creative reset)
- Independent bookings cluster around **big weekends** (WrestleMania weekend has become the
  industry's de facto convention: dozens of promotions run in the host city over four days,
  and it is simultaneously the biggest indie payday of the year and a giant informal
  scouting event)
- International recruitment follows **tour schedules** — a promotion scouting Japan does it
  around the major tours

### 5.4 WrestleMania weekend as an institution
Worth calling out separately. Since roughly 2015, the Thursday–Sunday around WrestleMania
has become a floating wrestling convention in the host city: 30–60 independent shows,
every notable indie promotion, thousands of travelling fans, and every talent scout in the
business. It is:
- The biggest indie revenue weekend of the year by a wide margin
- The most efficient scouting environment that exists
- Where a large fraction of the industry's networking, deal-making and reputation-building
  happens

## 6. The offer and the negotiation

### 6.1 What is on the table
- **Term** (1–5 years; developmental usually 3 with options)
- **Downside guarantee** (a minimum annual payment regardless of dates worked)
- **Per-date fees** above the guarantee, or a pure guarantee at higher levels
- **Merchandise royalty** percentage
- **Creative control clauses** (extremely rare, historically granted to a handful of
  major stars and always controversial)
- **Booking exclusivity** and whether outside dates are permitted
- **Non-compete** on termination (typically 90 days at Tier 0)
- **Injury clauses** — whether the contract tolls (freezes and extends) during injury.
  **This is a bigger deal than the salary for many performers** and is a standard feature
  at Tier 0.
- **Downside protection, medical coverage, travel policy**

See [11-contracts-and-talent-money.md](11-contracts-and-talent-money.md) for detail.

### 6.2 Leverage
A performer's leverage comes from:
1. **A competing offer** (the only real leverage)
2. **Being demonstrably over** (merch, crowd reaction, a segment that moved the number)
3. **Timing** — negotiating when the company needs to look strong
4. **Independence** — having an income that isn't the promotion (podcast, other bookings,
   acting, a business)
5. **Irreplaceability** — being the only person who can do what you do

A performer with none of these has no leverage at all, which is the ordinary condition of
most of a roster and is why the industry's labour economics are what they are.

## 7. Who doesn't get hired, and why it matters

Systematic biases in wrestling recruitment, stated plainly because they are real and
consequential:
- **Size bias.** Persistent, historically extreme, and repeatedly falsified by audiences.
- **Look conformity.** Bodybuilder aesthetics were near-mandatory for decades.
- **Age.** Almost no Tier 0 developmental signings over 30 without existing name value —
  even though many great performers peaked in their late 30s.
- **Gender.** The women's divisions were structurally under-invested for most of wrestling
  history — smaller rosters, shorter matches, fewer titles, less TV. This changed
  substantially from ~2015 onward, but the historical baseline matters when modelling any
  era before it.
- **Race and nationality.** Documented history of gimmick-based typecasting and glass
  ceilings at the top of the card.
- **Charisma-blindness.** The most consequential *practical* bias: recruiters can measure
  a vertical leap and cannot measure whether a crowd will care, so they hire for what they
  can measure.

## Sim implications

- **Hiring should be a multi-source funnel**, not a market list: indies, athletic converts,
  legacies, international, poaching, and returns should have different cost, risk, and
  outcome profiles.
- **Scouting should be imperfect information.** The player sees look, athleticism and
  in-ring readily; connection/charisma should be **noisy or hidden until the performer is in
  front of a crowd**. That single design choice reproduces the industry's central drama.
- **The hiring windows** (§5.1) give the game a natural rhythm and make the release-wave
  → signing-spree cycle an emergent, recognisable event.
- **Developmental systems as distinct institutions** with different outputs: a PC-style
  academy (high floor, homogenised, low ceiling on character) vs a dojo/excursion system
  (slow, high character output) vs indie-scouting (variable, cheap, needs no facility).
- **The excursion mechanic** (§4.3) is a beautiful, underused idea for a sim: send a
  developmental talent away for a year, they return repackaged with improved stats and a
  freshness reset.
- **Decision-maker taste** as an actual modelled bias — an owner who over-weights look
  should produce a visibly different roster over ten in-game years than one who
  over-weights workrate. That is more interesting than a neutral optimiser.
