# 09 — Revenue and Cost Model

*Where the money comes from and where it goes, at every tier. The financial skeleton
underneath every creative decision.*

---

## 1. The revenue lines

Every promotion in history has drawn from some subset of these seven:

| # | Line | Who it works for | Margin | Volatility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Media rights** | Tier 0–2 | Very high | Low within a term, cliff-edge at renewal |
| 2 | **Live gate** | All tiers | Low–medium | High |
| 3 | **Direct consumer** (PPV, subscription, VOD) | Tier 0–3 | High | Medium |
| 4 | **Merchandise** | All tiers | High | Medium; concentrated in a few performers |
| 5 | **Licensing** (toys, games, apparel, film) | Tier 0 mainly | Very high | Low |
| 6 | **Sponsorship / advertising** | Tier 0–2 | High | Medium |
| 7 | **Site fees / appearance fees** | Tier 0–1 | Extremely high | Deal-dependent |

### 1.1 Approximate WWE revenue mix (mid-2020s, standalone before/within TKO)
```
  Media rights & content         ~62%
  Live events (gate)             ~11%
  Consumer products (merch,
    licensing, games)            ~13%
  Sponsorship & advertising       ~9%
  Other (site-fee-adjacent,
    misc.)                        ~5%
```
Compare to **1990**:
```
  Live events                    ~55%
  PPV                            ~20%
  Merchandise & licensing        ~15%
  Television                     ~10%
```

**That inversion is the story of modern wrestling.** In 1990 the crowd paid the bills; in
2025 the network does. Everything about how the product is made follows from that.

### 1.2 A Tier 1 (AEW-type) mix, estimated
```
  Media rights                   ~55–65%
  Live gate                      ~15–20%
  PPV                            ~10–15%
  Merchandise                     ~8–12%
  Sponsorship                     ~3–5%
```
Similar shape to Tier 0 but with far less licensing and no site-fee income, and — crucially
— a much smaller absolute number against a talent cost base that is not proportionally
smaller.

### 1.3 A Tier 3/4 indie mix
```
  Live gate                      ~50–65%
  Merchandise (promotion's)      ~10–15%
  VIP / meet-and-greet           ~10–20%
  Streaming/VOD                   ~5–15%
  Sponsorship (local)             ~0–5%
```
No guaranteed income at all. Every show is an independent financial event.

## 2. The cost lines

| Line | Tier 0 share | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Talent costs** | 25–35% of revenue | The largest single controllable cost |
| **Television production** | 15–25% | Trucks, crew, sets, post |
| **Live event operations** | 8–15% | Venues, labour, security, ring crew |
| **Travel** | 5–10% | Enormous at high show counts |
| **Corporate overhead / SG&A** | 15–25% | Offices, legal, marketing, admin |
| **Content/library ops** | 2–5% | |
| **Capex** | Variable | HQ, training facility, trucks, sets |

### 2.1 Why talent cost is the pressure point
It is:
- The **largest** discretionary line
- The **most visible** to a CFO or an analyst
- The **easiest** to cut quickly (contracts have terms; releases are immediate)
- The **hardest** to link to revenue causally (which of these 160 people is drawing?)

That combination is precisely why release waves happen (see
[13-releases-and-firings.md](13-releases-and-firings.md)) and why they cluster around
quarter boundaries and ownership changes rather than creative moments.

### 2.2 The "roster larger than the television" problem
A promotion with 160 contracted performers and 4 hours of weekly television can feature
perhaps 40–60 of them meaningfully. The rest are:
- Paid a guarantee
- Not on television
- Losing value (a wrestler nobody sees is a wrestler nobody will pay to see)
- Simultaneously **not available to competitors**, which is the actual strategic reason for
  the oversized roster

**This is a defensive-signing strategy with an accounting cost**, and it is the central
financial criticism of both WCW in 1999–2000 and AEW in 2022–2024. It is not irrational —
denying a competitor a star has real value — but it is expensive and it demoralises the
people being warehoused.

## 3. Unit economics by activity

### 3.1 A televised weekly episode
```
  REVENUE
    Rights fee (allocated per episode)     $1,500,000–$8,000,000  [Tier 0]
    Gate (net)                                $150,000–$400,000
    Sponsorship (in-show)                      $50,000–$300,000
  COSTS
    Production (truck, crew, set, post)       $400,000–$1,200,000
    Venue & operations                         $80,000–$250,000
    Talent (appearance + travel)              $400,000–$900,000
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  NET: strongly positive, driven almost entirely by the rights fee
```

### 3.2 A premium event (streaming era)
No direct revenue attribution — it is a **retention and acquisition instrument**. Costs are
much higher (bigger set, longer show, bigger payoffs, more production), and the return is
measured in subscriptions kept, brand health, and site fees where applicable.

### 3.3 A house show
See [07-live-events-and-touring.md](07-live-events-and-touring.md) §6. Thin margins at
Tier 0, primary income at Tier 4.

### 3.4 The merchandise economics
- **Manufacturing cost** of a t-shirt: $4–8. **Retail:** $30–45. Gross margin 75–85%.
- **Splits:** venue takes 15–35% in-arena; talent royalty typically **a percentage of net**
  on their own items; e-commerce carries fulfilment cost but no venue split.
- **Concentration:** merchandise sales are extremely top-heavy. A handful of performers
  (historically Austin, Rock, Cena, Roman, Bryan, Punk, Rhea, Jey Uso in their respective
  eras) generate a very large share of total merch. **The #1 merch mover typically sells
  more than the bottom 40 combined.**
- **Why this matters for booking:** merch is the **least deniable** evidence of who is over.
  Ratings are confounded, crowds are local, but a person who moves shirts is a person the
  audience will spend money on. Multiple pushes in history have been triggered by merch
  numbers overriding management's preference.

## 4. The financial shape of each tier

### Tier 0
- Revenue $1.0–1.4B; EBITDA margins in the 30–40% range in strong years
- Effectively unkillable by wrestling-business factors alone
- The risk profile is **regulatory, reputational, and corporate-strategic**, not operational

### Tier 1
- Revenue $150–250M against costs that can exceed it
- **Survives on the owner's willingness to fund losses.** This is a completely different
  risk model from Tier 0 and it means the promotion's lifespan is a function of one
  person's patience.
- The single biggest financial question: does the next TV deal go up, flat, or down? A
  flat-or-down renewal at Tier 1 is close to an extinction event, because the cost base
  was built assuming growth.

### Tier 2
- Revenue $20–80M; usually operating near break-even or modest profit
- Survives through discipline, a stable domestic audience, and low cost structure
- Vulnerable to talent raids from Tiers 0/1 (the raid costs them nothing and costs Tier 2
  its top of the card)

### Tier 3
- Revenue $1–15M; profit is real but small
- **Very high operating leverage** — one hot year can double revenue, one bad one halves it
- The typical death: overexpansion after a good year

### Tier 4/5
- Revenue $50k–$1M
- Frequently subsidised by a wrestling school, the promoter's day job, or merchandising
- Death by: one bad show, a venue loss, a key worker leaving, or the promoter's life
  changing

## 5. The recurring financial mistakes

1. **Expanding the cost base on a temporary revenue spike.** The single most common death.
   A hot year produces new signings, a bigger production, and more TV — all of which are
   fixed costs when the spike ends. WCW 1998–2000 and TNA circa 2010 are the textbook cases.
2. **Signing talent defensively without a plan to use them.** Cost with no revenue and a
   demoralised roster.
3. **Buying names instead of building them.** A 45-year-old star's name value is a
   depreciating asset you are renting at peak price.
4. **Chasing a rival's spending.** WCW's 1999 payroll escalation was a response to WWF, not
   to its own economics.
5. **Under-investing in production.** Cheap-looking television devalues everything on it,
   and the audience judges production quality within seconds.
6. **Treating the gate as the goal at Tier 0/1.** Optimising for a full building at the
   expense of the television product is optimising the small line at the expense of the
   large one.
7. **Ignoring merch data.** The clearest signal in the business, routinely subordinated to
   the decision-maker's taste.
8. **Running too many towns.** Saturating markets to hit a show count destroys the towns
   for years.

---

## 6. What "financially healthy" looks like

- **Guaranteed revenue covers fixed costs.** Everything else is upside.
- **No single revenue line above ~65%** — concentration risk is real (see WCW's dependence
  on Turner)
- **Talent cost tracks revenue** rather than ambition
- **Market depth**: 25+ towns that reliably draw, so no single market's decline is fatal
- **A star pipeline**, so the top of the card is not a depreciating asset
- **A library**, which is a real appreciating asset and a hedge

## 7. Warning signs of financial trouble (visible from outside)

| Sign | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| Release waves outside normal cycles | Cost pressure |
| Reduced touring / cancelled house-show loops | Gate not covering costs |
| Cheaper-looking sets and fewer pyro/production elements | Production budget cut |
| Heavy reliance on part-time returning names | Attempting to buy attention instead of building it |
| Longer matches, more talking segments | Cheap content to fill time |
| Repeated "biggest ever" claims | Marketing compensating for a soft product |
| Moving a show to a worse time slot | The network's valuation has fallen |
| Selling or licensing the library | Monetising the last asset |
| Papering houses aggressively | Attendance collapse being concealed |

## 8. The two financial models that actually exist

Almost every promotion runs one of these, and confusing them is fatal:

### Model A — The media company
Revenue is a guaranteed rights fee. The show exists to be broadcast. The crowd is set
dressing. **Success = renewal at a higher fee.** Optimise for: consistency, demo delivery,
brand safety, star recognisability.

### Model B — The live-event company
Revenue is tickets and merchandise. The show exists to be attended. **Success = full
buildings that stay full.** Optimise for: satisfying the people in the room, delivering
advertised cards, building local habit.

**These optimise for opposite things**, and a promotion in transition between them (which
WWE was 2014–2019, and every growing indie is) suffers from doing both badly. The classic
symptom is a company producing television for a mass audience it doesn't have while
alienating the live audience it does.

## Sim implications

- Implement a **real P&L** with the seven revenue lines and the cost lines above. The
  numbers here are directly usable as starting coefficients.
- **Model A vs Model B** as a strategic identity that changes what the game scores you on
  is a strong structural idea — the same booking decision is correct under one and wrong
  under the other.
- **Merchandise concentration** (top performer > bottom 40 combined) makes merch a
  meaningful, readable feedback signal — and a great way to surface "the audience likes
  someone you're not pushing".
- **The oversized-roster trap** is an excellent strategic tension: signing a rival's star
  denies them value but costs you money and TV time you don't have.
- **The expansion-on-a-spike death spiral** (§5.1) is the most important economic failure
  to model. It should be tempting, look correct at the time, and be very hard to reverse.
- **Warning signs** (§7) are a great source of in-game news/flavour that also communicates
  real state to the player.
