# 11 — Contracts and Talent Money

*What wrestlers are actually paid, how deals are structured, and why the labour economics
of wrestling look the way they do.*

---

## 1. The foundational legal fact: independent contractors

In the United States, wrestlers at essentially every promotion — including WWE — are
contracted as **independent contractors**, not employees.

**What that means in practice:**
- No employer-provided health insurance (WWE covers in-ring injuries and medical for
  injuries sustained at work, but not general health insurance)
- No pension, no 401(k) match, no unemployment insurance
- Performers pay their own travel, accommodation, gear, and (historically) their own
  transport between towns
- Self-employment tax burden falls on the performer
- No collective bargaining; **wrestling has never been unionised**, despite repeated
  attempts (the most famous being Jesse Ventura's 1980s effort, which failed after it was
  reported to management)

**Why it is contested:** the contracts simultaneously impose exclusivity, control over
schedule, control over the performer's name and likeness, control over outside bookings and
appearances, and mandatory participation — a level of control that is characteristic of
employment. This has been the subject of litigation and legislative attention (notably a
2016 class action, and periodic state-level proposals). As of the mid-2020s the
classification stands.

**Why it matters for modelling:** it makes talent a **variable cost that can be cut
instantly**, which is the root of the release-wave dynamic, and it makes performers bear
the risk of injury, travel, and career length personally.

## 2. Contract structures by tier

### 2.1 Tier 0 — the guaranteed downside deal
The standard WWE main-roster structure:

| Component | Typical form |
|---|---|
| **Downside guarantee** | An annual minimum paid regardless of how much you work |
| **Per-appearance fees** | Earned against the guarantee; you only see money above it if your appearances exceed it |
| **PPV/PLE bonuses** | Historically a share of event revenue based on card position; the streaming transition changed this to a flat-ish structure |
| **Merchandise royalty** | A percentage of net sales of your own merchandise (commonly cited in the ~10–15% range, structure varies) |
| **Term** | 3–5 years typical |
| **Tolling clause** | Contract freezes and extends during injury or absence — you cannot run out the clock while hurt |
| **90-day non-compete** | On release, you cannot work elsewhere for 90 days (often paid) |
| **Likeness/IP** | The promotion owns ring names, gimmicks, and characters it creates; the performer's real name is theirs |
| **Exclusivity** | Total for wrestling; carve-outs for acting, appearances, conventions with approval |

**Approximate downside ranges (mid-2020s, WWE, widely reported rather than disclosed):**

| Position | Annual downside |
|---|---|
| Developmental (PC/NXT) | $50,000–$100,000 |
| Main roster, lower card | $150,000–$300,000 |
| Main roster, mid-card | $300,000–$700,000 |
| Upper card / champion tier | $1M–$3M |
| Top stars | $3M–$5M+ |
| Special attractions (part-time, high-value) | $5M–$10M+ per year for very few dates |

*Confidence: these are aggregated from reporting, court filings, and performer statements
over years. Treat as order-of-magnitude, not precise.*

**Total earnings** for a top performer include merchandise, PLE bonuses, video game and
licensing royalties, external appearances, and (for a few) outside media income, which can
multiply the downside substantially.

### 2.2 Tier 1 — bigger guarantees, fewer dates
AEW's structure is reported as more guarantee-heavy with fewer required dates. This is a
deliberate recruiting advantage: **more money for less work and more creative freedom** was
the founding pitch. It is also the source of the roster-cost problem — a large roster of
guaranteed deals against limited television.

### 2.3 Tier 2 — mixed
Some guaranteed contracts for top talent, a lot of per-date and per-tour arrangements.
Japanese promotions historically used yearly contracts with a dojo/loyalty structure and a
tour-based appearance schedule; foreign talent are typically booked per-tour.

### 2.4 Tier 3/4/5 — per-date
- **Tier 3 super-indie:** $300–$2,000 per date for a name; $150–$500 for a regular
- **Tier 4 regional:** $50–$300 per date; sometimes gas money and a spot on the card
- **Tier 5:** frequently nothing, or a share of the door
- **Plus travel and accommodation**, which the performer often pays themselves — meaning a
  $200 booking 400 miles away can be a net loss

**Merchandise is the difference.** An indie performer selling their own shirts at the
gimmick table can make $200–$1,000 a night on merch, often exceeding the booking fee. This
is why building a direct audience relationship is the entire economic strategy of an
independent career.

### 2.5 The realistic annual income picture
| Level | Realistic annual income from wrestling |
|---|---|
| Tier 5 / rookie | $0–$5,000 (net negative in year one after training and travel) |
| Tier 4 regular | $5,000–$25,000 — **requires a day job** |
| Tier 3 established indie name | $40,000–$150,000 (bookings + merch + bookings abroad) |
| Elite indie / international freelancer | $150,000–$400,000 |
| Tier 1/2 contracted | $100,000–$500,000 |
| Tier 0 main roster | $250,000–$1M |
| Tier 0 top of the card | $2M–$10M+ |

**The distribution is extremely top-heavy.** The great majority of working professional
wrestlers earn under $25,000 a year from wrestling. This is the single most important
labour fact in the industry.

## 3. What the contract controls

Beyond money, a Tier 0 contract typically governs:
- **Schedule** — you go where and when you are told
- **Character and name** — the promotion owns what it creates. This is why released
  performers change their names, and why some performers fight hard to trademark their
  own names before signing.
- **Outside appearances** — conventions, signings, podcasts, other media require approval
- **Third-party monetisation** — WWE's 2020 restriction on Twitch/Cameo/third-party
  platforms was a significant and contentious assertion of this control, and directly
  motivated some departures
- **Social media** — content guidelines, sometimes account ownership disputes
- **Medical** — mandatory clearance, mandatory testing, mandatory treatment through
  company-approved providers for in-ring injuries
- **Conduct** — a morals/conduct clause allowing termination for reputational harm

---

## 4. Creative control clauses

The rarest and most contested contract term: the right to refuse a creative direction
(most famously, to refuse to lose).

- Granted to a very small number of performers historically (the canonical examples are
  from WCW's late-1990s signings — Hogan, Nash, Hall and others were reported to have
  varying forms of creative control or approval).
- **The effect on a promotion is corrosive out of proportion to the number of clauses.**
  If two or three people cannot be beaten, the entire card above them is frozen, no new
  star can be made through them, and every other performer knows it.
- WCW's collapse is substantially attributable to this: a top tier that could not lose, in
  a business whose only mechanism for creating value is having people beat other people.

**Modern equivalents** are softer and mostly informal: veterans with the standing to
"have a conversation" about a finish, or contracts with consultation (not approval) rights.

## 5. How performers make money outside the contract

| Source | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Merchandise royalties** | The biggest variable for a popular performer |
| **Independent bookings** | Where permitted (Tier 1 often permits, Tier 0 rarely) |
| **Conventions and signings** | A significant income line, especially for retired performers |
| **Cameo / personalised video** | Meaningful money for recognisable names; restricted at some promotions |
| **Podcasts and YouTube** | A genuinely large parallel economy; several retired performers earn more from podcasting than they did wrestling |
| **Acting and media** | The top of the exit ramp (Rock, Cena, Batista, Ronda Rousey) |
| **Coaching and schools** | The most common post-career path |
| **International tours** | Japanese and European tours pay well for name US talent |
| **Sponsorship** | Rare and mostly for the very top |

## 6. The economics from the promotion's side

### 6.1 Why guarantees exist
- **Exclusivity requires compensation.** You can't stop someone working elsewhere without
  paying them for the time.
- **Retention.** A guarantee reduces the probability of losing someone mid-story.
- **Planning.** You need to know who is available.

### 6.2 Why guarantees are dangerous
- They convert a variable cost into a fixed one
- They accumulate: a promotion signs defensively during a hot period and carries the cost
  through a cold one
- They **decouple pay from performance**, which means a performer who is not being used
  still costs full price

### 6.3 The "warehousing" problem
Signing talent primarily to deny them to a rival. Costs:
- Direct salary
- The performer's value depreciating from lack of exposure
- Morale damage across the roster (everyone can see who isn't being used)
- The eventual release, at which point the rival gets them anyway, now cheaper and angrier

## 7. Negotiation dynamics

### 7.1 When a performer has power
- A **competing offer in hand** — the only genuinely reliable leverage
- **Contract expiry approaching during a hot run** — the timing that produced most of the
  industry's biggest paydays
- **Being irreplaceable** in a current storyline
- **Independent income** that makes walking away survivable
- **Public sentiment** — an audience that would react badly to losing them

### 7.2 When a promotion has power
- The performer has **no alternative at comparable money** (the normal case)
- The performer is **injured** (tolling extends the deal)
- The performer is **young and unproven**
- **Market concentration** — when only one or two employers exist, wages are suppressed
  structurally. The existence of a genuine competitor is the single biggest determinant of
  wrestler pay across the industry's history. Compare wages during the Monday Night Wars
  (1996–2001) and after WCW's death (2001–2019).

### 7.3 The "we'll see you in 90 days" dynamic
A released performer with a 90-day non-compete cannot immediately sign elsewhere, which:
- Gives the releasing promotion time to reconsider
- Gives the market time to cool
- Creates a predictable calendar of availability that rivals plan around

## 8. Compensation history, briefly (for era modelling)

| Era | Model |
|---|---|
| **Territory era (pre-1984)** | Percentage of the gate, paid nightly in cash. A main-eventer in a good territory made real money; everyone else scraped. No guarantees, no benefits, no security. |
| **National expansion (1984–1995)** | Guarantees appear for top stars; merchandising becomes significant; the middle of the card is still per-date |
| **Monday Night Wars (1996–2001)** | **The best time in history to be a wrestler.** Two well-funded bidders, escalating guarantees, creative control clauses, and genuine leverage |
| **Monopoly era (2002–2018)** | Wages compress. One major employer. Downside guarantees become standard but modest. |
| **New competition (2019–)** | Wages rise again, particularly in the middle of the card, because there is somewhere else to go |

**The pattern is unambiguous: wrestler compensation is a direct function of the number of
credible employers.** Not of the industry's revenue, not of the product's quality — of
competition for labour.

## 9. What performers actually complain about

Consistently, across decades and promotions, and useful as a model of talent satisfaction:
1. **Creative direction** — not being used, being used badly, having a character taken away
2. **Not knowing** — finding out your segment is cut at 6pm; no communication about plans
3. **Travel and schedule** — the physical cost
4. **Money relative to what the company earns**
5. **Health coverage and career length**
6. **Being lied to** — the single most damaging thing a promotion can do to its roster's
   morale. Broken promises about pushes are cited more than money in almost every
   post-career account.
7. **Political disadvantage** — someone else's friend getting the spot

Note that **money is fourth**. Creative use and honest communication rank higher, which is
why talent relations competence (see [03](03-roles-and-competence.md) §7.6) affects
retention more than payroll does.

## Sim implications

- **Contract objects** should carry: term, guarantee, per-date rate, merch %, exclusivity,
  tolling, non-compete, and any creative-control clause. Each of these is a lever with
  distinct consequences.
- **Creative control clauses** are a superb high-risk mechanic: they let you sign a star you
  otherwise couldn't, and they permanently constrain your booking. The WCW failure mode
  should be reproducible.
- **Talent satisfaction should be multi-factor and weighted like §9** — creative use and
  honesty above money. A game where you can keep a roster happy purely by paying them is
  modelling the wrong industry.
- **Competition determines wages**: a global "number of credible employers" variable that
  moves the entire wage market is both historically accurate and creates a real strategic
  cost to a rival's existence.
- **The warehousing trade-off** (sign to deny) and the **90-day non-compete** are both clean,
  implementable mechanics with real strategic texture.
- Indie-tier economics (booking fee vs travel vs merch table) would make a
  start-at-the-bottom career or promotion mode genuinely tense.
