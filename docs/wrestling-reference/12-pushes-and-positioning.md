# 12 — Pushes and Positioning

*How the card hierarchy forms, how people move up and down it, and what actually causes
each.*

---

## 1. The card hierarchy

Every promotion has a position ladder, whether or not it says so out loud:

| Position | Function | Typical share of a roster |
|---|---|---|
| **Main event** | Draws the house, headlines, holds the world title | 4–8 people |
| **Upper card** | Credible challengers, secondary titles, big-match capable | 8–15 |
| **Mid-card** | The body of the show; feuds that fill TV; the pool from which the next upper card comes | 20–40 |
| **Lower card** | Regular TV, mostly losing, occasional showcase | 20–40 |
| **Enhancement** | Loses to establish others ("jobber", "enhancement talent") | Varies; near-zero at Tier 0 today, standard historically |

**Position is not a job title; it is the audience's belief.** A performer is main event
when the audience believes they could beat anyone and would pay to see it. Management can
place someone in the main event slot; only the audience can make them a main eventer.

This distinction — **booked position vs perceived position** — is the single most useful
concept in this document. Every "failed push" in history is a gap between the two.

### 1.1 In this repo
`CardPosition` is derived from popularity rather than stored, which is the right instinct —
position should be an *output* of audience perception, not an input. The refinement worth
making is that the *booking* position and the *perceived* position should be two different
values, and the tension between them should drive outcomes.

---

## 2. What a push actually is

A **push** is a sustained allocation of the promotion's scarce resources to one performer:
- **Wins**, especially over people the audience respects
- **Television time**, especially in prominent slots
- **Promo time** and character development
- **Match time** with the roster's best workers
- **Presentation** — entrance production, commentary emphasis, graphics, merchandise
- **Proximity** to established stars

A push is expensive because all of those are zero-sum. Pushing someone means someone else
loses, gets less time, and is diminished. **This is why pushes are political** — they are
resource allocation, and every allocation has losers who know they lost.

## 3. The mechanics of elevation

### 3.1 The canonical star-making sequence
(Repeated from [04-booking-philosophy.md](04-booking-philosophy.md) because it is the
central mechanism.)

1. **Exposure** — get them on screen with someone the audience cares about
2. **Survival** — they hang, take a beating, don't get embarrassed
3. **The moral victory** — a count-out, a DQ, a near-fall the crowd bought
4. **The tainted win** — they win with help, a fluke, or a distraction
5. **The clean win** — over a credible name, decisively
6. **The moment** — a big win on the biggest stage, with the whole company watching

Timeline: **6–18 months**, sometimes longer. Skipping steps produces rejection.

### 3.2 The accelerants
- **A great opponent.** The fastest way to make someone is to have a top star work with
  them at length and lose. This is the traditional "putting someone over" and it is the
  single most effective star-making tool that exists.
- **A defining moment.** One image, one spot, one promo that people remember and share.
- **A crowd revolt.** When the audience adopts someone independently of management.
- **A great gimmick change.** A character that suddenly fits.
- **A title win at the right time.**
- **Absence and return.** A repackage or a return after injury/departure.

### 3.3 The killers
- **Winning without meaning.** Beating people the audience doesn't rate does nothing.
- **The 50/50 loop.** Trading wins with a peer forever; both stay exactly where they were.
- **Being protected.** Never losing but never beating anyone credible reads as fragile.
- **Losing the crowd's belief in the presentation.** Bad music, bad gear, being called
  "a young man with a lot of potential" on commentary for two years.
- **Getting talked past.** Commentary that treats the performer as filler.
- **Being kept off television.** Absence without a story is just absence; the audience
  forgets. Two months invisible undoes six months of build.

## 4. The forms of push, and their failure modes

### 4.1 The slow burn
Gradual elevation over a year+. **Best success rate.** The audience is brought along and
believes it discovered the person. *(Daniel Bryan 2012–2014, Roman Reigns' Tribal Chief
rebuild 2020–2023, Okada 2012–2013.)*

**Failure mode:** the promotion loses its nerve halfway, or the performer plateaus, or the
booker changes and the plan dies.

### 4.2 The rocket push
Rapid elevation, weeks not months. Works when the performer is genuinely exceptional and
the audience reacts immediately. *(Goldberg 1997–98; Brock Lesnar 2002; Ronda Rousey 2018.)*

**Failure mode:** the audience hasn't been given a reason. Rejection is fast, loud, and
often permanent, because the performer is now associated with being force-fed. The
distinguishing variable is whether the audience *had already decided* before the push
started — a rocket push confirming an existing reaction works; a rocket push creating one
usually doesn't.

### 4.3 The coronation
A performer is announced, through presentation and booking, as The Guy. Everything is
arranged around them.

**Failure mode:** the most notorious in modern wrestling. When an audience senses it is
being *told* who to care about rather than being *persuaded*, the reaction is not apathy —
it is active resistance, and it escalates the longer management holds the line. The
2014–2016 Roman Reigns situation is the definitive case study and is worth understanding
mechanically: the performer was talented, the audience's rejection was about the *process*,
and the eventual solution (a heel turn, a repackage, absence, and a character built from
his real family lineage) worked completely.

**The lesson:** an audience will accept almost anyone if it is allowed to feel it chose
them, and will reject almost anyone if it feels overruled.

### 4.4 The organic push (crowd-led)
The audience picks someone and will not stop. Management follows. *(Austin 1996–97,
Bryan 2013–14, LA Knight 2023, Jey Uso 2023.)*

**Highest success rate of any push type**, and the hardest to fake. The signal is
unmistakable: reactions for someone who is not being featured, chants during other people's
segments, merch sales for someone who isn't in the main event.

**Failure mode:** management resists, or accepts too late, after the crowd has moved on.
There is a **window**, typically 3–9 months, in which an organic reaction can be
capitalised on. After that the audience gets tired of asking.

### 4.5 The repackage
Same performer, new character. Extremely effective when the original character was the
problem rather than the person. *(Rocky Maivia → The Rock; Ringmaster → Stone Cold; Husky
Harris → Bray Wyatt; countless Japanese excursion returns.)*

**Failure mode:** repackaging someone the audience has already rejected as a *person*
rather than a *character*, or too many repackages, at which point the audience stops
investing in any version.

### 4.6 The lateral push
Moving someone from one division/title to another to freshen them. Cheap, useful, limited.

## 5. Demotion — how people go down

Far less discussed and equally systematic.

### 5.1 The mechanics
- **Losing to people below them.** The fastest and clearest.
- **Losing television time.** Slipping from segment 2 to segment 7 to backstage-only.
- **Losing the belt without a rematch.**
- **Commentary de-emphasis.**
- **Being put in comedy.** The classic soft demotion — nearly irreversible, because a
  performer the audience laughs at is very hard to make threatening again.
- **Being paired with someone cold.**
- **Being taken off television entirely.**

### 5.2 Why people get demoted
| Reason | Notes |
|---|---|
| **The push failed** | The audience didn't take |
| **Injury** | Time away; the spot got filled |
| **Someone better arrived** | Zero-sum |
| **Backstage issue** | Attitude, reliability, a political loss |
| **They got stale** | Freshness decay; see [17](17-heat-and-getting-over.md) |
| **Contract situation** | A performer with an expiring deal often stops being featured |
| **Decision-maker changed** | A new booker has different favourites; this is the most common cause of sudden, unexplained demotions |
| **Age** | The slow, quiet one |

### 5.3 "Burial"
Fan term for a demotion perceived as deliberate and disproportionate. Real phenomenon,
overused as a term. Genuine burial is: repeated decisive losses, comedic humiliation, and
commentary treating the performer as a joke — the combination is what makes it stick.

**The important structural point:** burial damages the promotion, not just the performer,
because everyone that performer previously beat is retroactively devalued. Status in
wrestling is a graph, not a list; damaging a node damages everything connected to it.

## 6. The hierarchy as a system

### 6.1 Status is transitive (and this is load-bearing)
If A beats B and B beats C, the audience treats A as above C. This means:
- **Every match result is a statement about the whole card**, not just two people
- **Protecting everyone destroys the graph.** If nobody loses decisively, the graph has no
  edges and no one has a position.
- **Beating a credible loser is the only way to gain status.** Which means the promotion
  must be willing to *have credible people lose* — the eternal, universal booking problem.

### 6.2 The "who needs it more" calculus
The core booking question in every match: **who gains more from winning than the other
loses?**
- An established star loses very little from one clean loss, if their position is secure
- A rising performer gains enormously
- Therefore the star should lose — and yet the star's instinct, the promotion's caution,
  and often the star's contract all push the other way

**A promotion's willingness to have its top act lose to a rising act is the single best
predictor of whether it will have stars in five years.** This is not a metaphor; it's the
mechanism.

### 6.3 The finite top
There are only so many main event spots and only so much TV time. A promotion cannot push
everyone. Attempting to results in nobody being pushed, because a push is defined by its
scarcity.

## 7. Titles as positioning instruments
See [21-championships.md](21-championships.md) for detail. Briefly:
- **The world title defines the top.** Whoever holds it is main event by definition, which
  is exactly why giving it to someone the audience hasn't accepted is so dangerous.
- **Secondary titles are elevation tools** when used as a step up and devaluation tools when
  used as a consolation prize.
- **Tag titles** are the most efficient way to elevate two people at once, and the most
  commonly wasted.

## 8. Reading whether a push is working

| Signal | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Reactions **growing** week over week | Working |
| Merch sales rising | Working, strongly |
| Crowd reacts to their *music* before they appear | Working — this is the clearest single indicator |
| Crowd is quiet | Not working yet, or not working |
| Crowd boos a babyface | **Ambiguous and critical:** they care. Could be a heel turn opportunity or a rejection of the booking. |
| Sarcastic chants / chanting for someone else during their segment | **Actively failing.** The audience has decided to resist. |
| "What?" / counting along / beach balls | The audience is entertaining itself. You have lost them. |
| Reactions flat but merch strong | The presentation is wrong, not the person |
| Reactions strong but merch flat | They're entertaining but not identity-forming |

**The diagnostic pair to watch: crowd reaction and merch.** When they disagree, the
disagreement tells you what to fix.

---

## 9. Timing — when pushes happen

- **After a big show**, when the previous top story concluded and slots opened
- **After an injury** to someone above them
- **During a build to a tentpole**, when the promotion needs a fresh challenger
- **When a contract is signed** — a new signing is at maximum novelty and gets pushed
  immediately, often at the expense of someone who's been there
- **When merch data becomes undeniable**
- **When a booker changes** — the fastest and largest reshuffles in the business

## Sim implications

- **Split booked position from perceived position.** The player books; the audience decides;
  the gap is where the game lives. This is arguably the single most valuable addition to
  WrestleSim's model.
- **Status as a graph, not a number.** Beating someone should transfer status along a
  win/loss graph, and the audience's belief in person C should fall when C's only notable
  wins are over people who now lose to everyone.
- **The five-step elevation sequence** as a tracked, gated progression, with audience
  rejection for skipping steps, would produce authentic push dynamics.
- **Push types as distinct strategies** with different cost, speed, and failure profiles
  (slow burn / rocket / coronation / organic / repackage) is a rich, readable design.
- **The organic push window** (3–9 months to capitalise on a crowd-led reaction, after
  which it decays) is a superb pressure mechanic and directly models the industry's most
  famous recurring mistake.
- **Demotion mechanics** should exist explicitly — comedy as a near-irreversible soft
  demotion is a great detail.
- **The "who needs it more" calculus** could be surfaced as an actual displayed estimate,
  making the central booking trade-off legible.
