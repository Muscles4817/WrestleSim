# 01 — The Industry Map

*What the business is, how it is stratified, and where any given promotion sits.*

---

## 1. What professional wrestling actually is, as a business

Professional wrestling is a **serialised live-performance business** whose product is
recurring emotional investment in a cast of characters, monetised through four channels:

1. **Live attendance** — tickets to see it in person
2. **Media rights** — someone paying to broadcast or stream it
3. **Direct consumer purchase** — pay-per-view, subscription, premium content
4. **Merchandise and licensing** — t-shirts, toys, video games, brand rentals

Everything a promotion does is an attempt to convert *attention* into *investment* and
*investment* into one of those four revenue lines. The failure mode at every tier is
identical: getting attention without converting it to investment (a viral moment nobody
buys a ticket for), or having investment with no channel to monetise it (a beloved indie
with no TV deal).

**The critical structural fact:** wrestling has no offseason and no natural end point. A
show does not conclude; it continues next week, forever. This produces the industry's
defining problem — **the content treadmill**. Roughly 250–300 hours of first-run
television per year at the top tier, all of which must contain enough novelty to keep an
audience that has, in many cases, been watching for thirty years.

## 2. The tier system

Promotions sort into recognisable tiers by a small number of variables. The tier
determines almost everything else — schedule, hiring, contracts, creative process,
risk tolerance.

### Tier 0 — The global major (currently one: WWE)
| Attribute | Value |
|---|---|
| Annual revenue | $1.0–1.4B standalone (WWE, 2023–2025 range) |
| Roster under contract | 150–200 including developmental |
| Full-time employees | 800–1,000 |
| Weekly first-run TV | 5+ hours |
| Annual major events | 12–16 |
| Annual total shows | 250–320 (incl. house shows) |
| Media rights value | $500M+/yr aggregate |
| Buildings | Arenas (8,000–18,000), stadiums for tentpoles |

Defining feature: **it does not need any single performer**. The brand outdraws the
roster. This is the only tier at which that is true, and it is the single most important
structural fact about WWE — it changes negotiating posture, creative risk tolerance, and
the entire talent-relations model.

### Tier 1 — The national challenger (currently: AEW; historically WCW, TNA at peak)
| Attribute | Value |
|---|---|
| Annual revenue | $150–250M |
| Roster under contract | 100–160 |
| Weekly first-run TV | 4–5 hours |
| Annual major events | 4–8 |
| Annual total shows | 100–140 |
| Buildings | Theatres to mid-arenas (2,500–12,000), occasional stadium |

Defining feature: **national television distribution but roster-dependent drawing power**.
Losing three top acts is an existential event. Tier 1 promotions are usually
**owner-funded** or **backed by a media partner**, and their survival is a function of the
patience of that funder rather than of profitability.

### Tier 2 — The established regional/international with real TV (NJPW, CMLL, AAA, historic mid-major territories, TNA/Impact in its later form, MLW)
| Attribute | Value |
|---|---|
| Annual revenue | $20–80M (NJPW is at the top of this and arguably its own tier) |
| Roster | 40–90 |
| TV | Domestic broadcast or streaming; often the promotion's own platform |
| Annual shows | 100–180 (NJPW runs a lot; others far fewer) |
| Buildings | 1,000–8,000, with 2–4 big-building tentpoles |

### Tier 3 — The super-indie / national indie (ROH pre-2022, PWG, GCW, Beyond, wXw, RevPro, DEFY, Progress at peak)
| Attribute | Value |
|---|---|
| Annual revenue | $1–15M |
| Roster | Almost all per-date bookings; 0–20 contracts |
| TV | Streaming platform, YouTube, or a niche network deal |
| Annual shows | 12–40 |
| Buildings | 300–2,500 |

Defining feature: **no talent lock-up**. The roster is shared with every other Tier 3
promotion, which is simultaneously the tier's greatest creative asset (dream matches,
cross-promotion) and its fatal weakness (no exclusivity, no equity in a star).

### Tier 4 — The regional independent
| Attribute | Value |
|---|---|
| Annual revenue | $50k–$1M |
| Roster | Per-date, mostly local, plus 1–2 "name" bookings per show |
| Annual shows | 6–24 |
| Buildings | 100–800 — VFW halls, armouries, fairgrounds, small theatres |

Defining feature: **the promoter's day job subsidises it**, or it runs at break-even.
Profit is per-show, in cash, on the night.

### Tier 5 — The local/hobbyist
Under 100 people in a room, run by a wrestling school as a graduation showcase or a
promoter with a ring in a barn. Economically negligible; ecologically essential — it is
where every single performer in every tier above started.

## 3. What actually separates the tiers

Not revenue — revenue is a symptom. The real dividing lines:

**Line 1: Does the company have guaranteed money coming in that is not attendance?**
(A television or streaming rights fee.) This separates Tiers 0–2 from 3–5 completely.
Without it, every show is an independent financial event and the company cannot plan.
With it, the company has a floor, can sign exclusive contracts, and can lose money on
individual shows in service of a longer story.

**Line 2: Is talent exclusive?**
Exclusivity is what converts a performer into an *asset*. A promotion that builds a star
it does not have under contract is doing free marketing for its competitors. This is the
single biggest structural reason super-indies cannot become majors on their own.

**Line 3: Is the brand bigger than the roster?**
Only WWE clears this. It means the company can lose anyone and continue. Everyone else is
running a business whose primary assets go home at night and can quit.

**Line 4: Can it run a building it does not have to fill?**
Tier 0 can run a 15,000-seat arena at 60% and still profit because the television is the
product and the crowd is set dressing. Tier 4 must fill the room or lose money.

## 4. The global landscape, by territory

### United States
- **WWE** (TKO Group Holdings; merged with UFC 2023, Endeavor/TKO structure). The market.
- **AEW** (Shahid Khan-funded, Warner Bros. Discovery TV). The challenger.
- **TNA** (formerly Impact; Anthem Sports). Tier 2, working relationship with WWE
  developmental as of the mid-2020s.
- **MLW, NWA, GCW, Beyond, DEFY, Wrestling Open, and hundreds of Tier 4s.**
- **Historical:** WCW (died 2001), ECW (died 2001), the NWA territory system (died
  1984–1990), AWA, WCCW, Memphis/CWA, Mid-South/UWF, Portland, Stampede (Canada).

### Japan (see [25-international-models.md](25-international-models.md))
- **NJPW** (Bushiroad-owned) — the largest non-US promotion, tour-based, strong-style
  lineage, January 4 Tokyo Dome as its WrestleMania.
- **AJPW, NOAH** (CyberAgent), **DDT** (CyberAgent), **Dragon Gate**, **Stardom**
  (Bushiroad, joshi), **TJPW**, **Big Japan**, **Michinoku Pro**.
- Structurally different: **tours**, not weekly episodic TV; **league tournaments** as the
  primary story engine; far less character-driven, far more won-loss-record-driven.

### Mexico
- **CMLL** (founded 1933, the oldest continuously operating promotion in the world) —
  Friday nights at Arena México, weekly, family-owned (the Lutteroth line).
- **AAA** (founded 1992, acquired by WWE in 2025) — more television-friendly, more
  spectacle.
- Structurally different: **lucha libre** conventions — mask culture, *máscara contra
  máscara* stipulations, trios as the base match format, *técnico/rudo* rather than
  face/heel, and a licensing/commission system (the Mexico City Boxing and Wrestling
  Commission) that has statutory authority over masks and identities.

### United Kingdom / Europe
- **Progress, RevPro, ICW, TNT, wXw (Germany), Westside Xtreme, PROGRESS**, plus the
  historical **Joint Promotions/World of Sport** era (ITV, 1955–1988) which was a genuine
  mass-audience broadcast product with its own round-based, points-scoring rules.
- WWE's **NXT UK** (2018–2022) briefly consolidated the scene and then vacated it, which
  is a case study in a major distorting a local ecosystem.

### Elsewhere
Australia (MCW, PWA), Canada (regional; historically Stampede and Maple Leaf), Germany,
India (a repeatedly attempted, repeatedly failed market for a domestic major), Saudi Arabia
(not a promotion but a **buyer** — see [09-revenue-and-costs.md](09-revenue-and-costs.md)
on site fees, which materially changed WWE's business from 2018).

## 5. The ecology — how the tiers feed each other

```
  Tier 5 schools ──► Tier 4 regionals ──► Tier 3 super-indies ──► Tier 1/2 ──► Tier 0
       │                   │                      │                   │           │
       └── training ───────┴──── reps, tape ───────┴───名 name value ──┴─ exclusivity
                                                                                   │
                                       returning veterans, releases ◄──────────────┘
```

Key dynamics:

- **Talent flows up; money flows down only through wages.** A Tier 4 promoter pays $150 to
  book a wrestler whose exposure came from a Tier 3 show streamed for free.
- **Releases flow down and are the lifeblood of Tiers 1–3.** A WWE release cycle (see
  [13-releases-and-firings.md](13-releases-and-firings.md)) instantly repopulates the
  independent scene with recognisable names, and every mid-tier promotion's business
  spikes for the following 6–12 months.
- **The majors do not develop talent from zero efficiently.** They develop *athletes*.
  The character work, the ability to work a crowd, and the comfort in front of 200 people
  are almost always learned in Tiers 4 and 5. When a major cuts off its indie pipeline it
  is running down a resource it did not create and cannot replace quickly. WWE
  discovering this circa 2019–2021 (the "Performance Center-only" recruiting era producing
  athletic performers who couldn't connect) and reversing course is the clearest recent
  example.
- **Every super-indie boom is followed by a major raiding it.** 2016–2019 indie boom →
  AEW forms from it and WWE signs against it → indie scene hollows → 2022–2024 rebuild.
  This cycle has run at least four times (territories→WWF 1984; ECW→WWF/WCW 1996–99;
  ROH/PWG→WWE/NXT 2013–2017; indie boom→AEW 2019).

## 6. Market size and reach — the honest numbers

Wrestling's self-image is much larger than its actual footprint. Useful calibration:

| Metric | Reality (mid-2020s) |
|---|---|
| WWE Raw viewership | 1.3–1.8M (Netflix era metrics differ; USA-era 1.5–2.0M) |
| WWE SmackDown | 2.0–2.5M |
| AEW Dynamite | 600k–900k |
| NXT | 550k–750k |
| WrestleMania gate | $15–25M+ per night (stadium, 2020s) |
| Biggest single-night gate ever | WrestleMania 39/40/41-era stadium nights, $20M+ |
| Peak wrestling TV (Attitude, 1999) | Raw + Nitro combined 9–11M weekly |
| Peak single show (Raw, 1999) | ~8.1M viewers, 6.9 rating |
| AEW peak (2019–2021) | ~1.4M |
| NJPW Tokyo Dome (Jan 4) | 25,000–40,000 depending on year and configuration |
| Typical Tier 4 indie house | 150–400 |

**The most important number in the industry:** wrestling's television audience declined by
roughly 75–80% from its 1999 peak to the mid-2020s, while revenue *increased* several-fold.
That divergence — fewer viewers, more money — is the defining economic fact of the modern
era and explains almost every strategic decision made since 2014. See
[08-television-and-media.md](08-television-and-media.md).

## 7. Why promotions exist at all (the founder motives)

Understanding why someone starts a promotion predicts how they will run it:

| Motive | Typical founder | Behaviour it produces | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Business** | Promoter/entrepreneur | Cost discipline, market focus, willingness to cut favourites | Under-investment in product; commodity feel |
| **Vanity / self-push** | An active wrestler | The owner is champion; creative bends to them | Roster resentment, ceiling at the owner's drawing power |
| **Ideology** ("wrestling should be X") | A purist or a disaffected ex-employee | Distinct, coherent product with a devoted core | Refuses to broaden; caps at the ideology's audience |
| **Legacy/family** | Inherited promotion | Continuity, tradition, deep local roots | Resistance to change, generational succession fights |
| **Mark money** | Wealthy fan | Overspends on names, underspends on production/marketing | Burns capital fast; typically 18–36 months |
| **Platform play** | Media company | Wrestling as content inventory for a network/streamer | Creative subordinated to scheduling; cancelled when the parent's strategy shifts |

The single most predictive question about any promotion: **whose money is it, and what do
they want it to do?** A promotion funded by someone who wants a return behaves nothing
like one funded by someone who wants to be in the wrestling business.

## Sim implications

- Promotion **tier** should be a first-class entity with derived properties: show cadence,
  contract types available, building sizes, ability to sign exclusives, media income floor.
- The four "dividing lines" (guaranteed income, exclusivity, brand-over-roster, ability to
  under-fill a building) are better tier tests than a raw revenue number — they produce
  categorically different gameplay rather than a slider.
- The **ecology** matters: released talent should flow downward and give lower tiers a
  temporary lift. A game where the major's roster churn has no effect on the rest of the
  world is missing the industry's main circulatory system.
- **Founder motive** is a strong candidate for a promotion-generation trait, because it
  produces coherent behavioural packages rather than random stat spreads.
