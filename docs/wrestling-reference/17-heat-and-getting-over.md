# 17 — Heat, Getting Over, and Getting Cold

*The mechanics of momentum: what makes a performer catch fire, what makes them cool, and
why it happens on the schedule it does.*

This answers the user questions: **"What makes people get hot? What makes people get cold?"**

---

## 1. Definitions, because the words are used loosely

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Over** | The audience cares about you. The fundamental state. Can be over as a face or as a heel. |
| **Heat** | (1) Audience hatred for a heel. (2) The accumulated tension in a feud. (3) Backstage trouble. Context disambiguates; in this reference, sense (2) unless stated. |
| **Hot** | Currently rising. Reactions growing, merch moving, the audience wants more. |
| **Cold** | Currently falling. Reactions shrinking, indifference growing. |
| **Getting over** | The process of becoming over |
| **Made** | Permanently over. Rare. The audience's relationship survives bad booking, absence, and time. |
| **Buried** | Deliberately diminished |
| **Cooled off** | Was hot, no longer is |
| **Draw** | Over *and* converts that into revenue. Not all over performers draw. |

### 1.1 The critical distinction: over vs hot
- **Over** is a *stock* — accumulated audience relationship. Slow to build, slow to lose.
- **Hot** is a *flow* — current momentum. Fast to gain, fast to lose.

A performer can be very over and cold (a beloved veteran nobody is currently excited about)
or hot and not very over (a new signing generating buzz with no depth of relationship yet).
**The two need separate variables.** Conflating them is the most common modelling error.

---

## 2. What makes someone get hot

### 2.1 The moment
A single memorable event that recontextualises a performer. Austin's Austin 3:16 promo
(1996), Foley's Hell in a Cell, the Rock's first true heel promos, Bryan's post-Rumble 2014
crowd revolt, Jey Uso's "Main Event" run, Rhea Ripley's Judgment Day repackage.

**Properties of a moment:**
- It is **specific and shareable** — one image or one line
- It reveals something the audience didn't know about the character
- It happens in front of a crowd that reacts, which validates it
- It is **not planned as a moment** more often than not

### 2.2 Being put over by a bigger star
The single most reliable *deliberate* method. A star loses to, or works a competitive match
with, someone below them, and transfers status. This is the mechanism the industry has
always used and it works because status is transitive (see
[12-pushes-and-positioning.md](12-pushes-and-positioning.md) §6.1).

### 2.3 The right character
A gimmick that fits the person. The transformation from a character imposed to a character
inhabited is often the whole difference. See
[19-characters-and-promos.md](19-characters-and-promos.md).

### 2.4 Winning something meaningful
A title, a tournament (Royal Rumble, G1, King of the Ring, Money in the Bank), a big match
on a big show. **Meaningful** is the operative word — winning something the audience doesn't
value does nothing.

### 2.5 Sympathy
Injury, injustice, betrayal, an unfair beating. The audience's protective instinct is one
of the strongest forces available and is cheap to trigger.

### 2.6 The turn
A well-timed alignment change. See [19](19-characters-and-promos.md) §6. A turn is the
single largest one-shot heat generator in wrestling.

### 2.7 Real-life bleeding into the fiction
The most potent and most dangerous tool. When the audience believes something is genuinely
true — a real grievance, a real contract situation, a real rivalry — investment jumps
dramatically. CM Punk's 2011 pipe bomb, Austin/McMahon's real power dynamic, the Montreal
fallout, the Bloodline's use of the Anoaʻi family's real relationships.

**Why it's dangerous:** you cannot control real life, the performers may not be able to
sustain it, and once the audience believes it's real, a "worked" resolution feels like a
betrayal.

### 2.8 Association with something hot
A faction, a hot feud, a hot show. Proximity transfers heat, imperfectly but reliably.
This is why factions are efficient elevation tools.

### 2.9 Absence and return
See §7.

### 2.10 An outstanding match
Works, but slower than the others. A great match builds *reputation*; reputation converts to
over-ness only with repetition. A performer who has three all-time great matches in a year
will be respected; whether they are over depends on whether the audience formed an
attachment to the person, not the performances.

### 2.11 The audience simply deciding
The most powerful mechanism and the least controllable. Sometimes an audience adopts
someone for reasons nobody planned — a look, a moment of realness, a chant that started as
a joke. When it happens, the only correct response is to go with it, quickly.

## 3. What makes someone get cold

### 3.1 Losing, without structure
Not losing itself — losing *without a story*. A performer who loses because they were
outnumbered, or in a story about their weakness, retains heat. A performer who just keeps
losing does not. **The audience will forgive any loss it understands.**

### 3.2 Overexposure
Being on television every week, doing the same thing, for too long. The audience's
attention is a resource and familiarity depletes it. This is the most common cause of
cooling and the least dramatic — nothing goes wrong, the reactions just get smaller.

### 3.3 Staleness of the character
The gimmick has said everything it has to say. The catchphrase is now background noise. The
audience knows exactly what will happen when the music hits.

### 3.4 Loss of novelty in the offence
Especially for high-spot performers. The dive that got a "holy shit" chant now gets polite
applause because the audience has seen it forty times. **Athletic novelty depletes faster
than character novelty**, which is why workrate-based over-ness is less durable than
character-based over-ness.

### 3.5 Being booked below their perceived level
If the audience thinks someone is a main eventer and they're wrestling in the second match,
the audience initially objects and then, crucially, **stops objecting**. That second phase
is the cooling.

### 3.6 The unpaid build
A feud with no blow-off. A promise not kept. The audience invests, gets nothing, and
withdraws — and withdraws faster the next time.

### 3.7 Comedy
A near-irreversible cooling agent for a serious character.

### 3.8 Bad matches with a bad opponent
Being repeatedly paired with someone the audience doesn't care about drags a performer down.
Heat transfer works in both directions.

### 3.9 Time off without a story
Absence *with* a story (injury angle, a departure, a suspension) generates anticipation.
Absence with no explanation generates forgetting. Roughly **6–10 weeks off television with
no narrative** and a mid-card performer's reactions measurably decline.

### 3.10 The audience deciding the promotion doesn't believe in them
Extremely subtle and extremely real. Commentary tone, entrance production, where they're
placed on the card, whether their merch exists. The audience reads all of it and calibrates
its investment to the promotion's own apparent investment.

### 3.11 Winning too much, too easily
Dominance without vulnerability produces respect without tension. If nobody can beat you,
your matches have no drama.

## 4. Freshness — the underlying variable

**Everything in wrestling decays.** Characters, catchphrases, moves, match-ups, factions,
stipulations. Freshness is the single most useful hidden variable in the business.

### 4.1 Typical decay periods
*Design estimates, useful as coefficients:*

| Thing | Fresh for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| A new character/gimmick | **6–18 months** | Then requires evolution or replacement |
| A catchphrase | **12–24 months** | Longer if the audience participates in it |
| A finisher | **Indefinite** if protected; loses impact if it stops finishing matches |
| A signature spot | **1–3 years** | Faster if imitated |
| A specific match-up (A vs B) | **2–4 encounters**, then needs a stipulation or a gap | The single fastest-decaying thing in wrestling |
| A faction | **12–24 months** | Then it must split, or add members, or become wallpaper |
| A stipulation match type | **1–2 per year, per promotion** | A cage match is special once a year |
| A returning legend | **1–3 appearances** | Then they're just a guy |
| A "shocking" swerve | **2–3 per year** | Then the audience expects them |
| A tag team | **2–4 years** | Then split them (and the split is a story) |

### 4.2 How freshness is restored
- **Absence** (the main tool)
- **Evolution of the character** — same person, new dimension
- **A turn**
- **A new context** — new opponent, new division, new promotion
- **A repackage** — new name, new look, new music
- **The excursion model** — leave, change, return
- **Time**, simply

### 4.3 The freshness ceiling on a roster
A promotion can only run so many characters before they blur. Practical limit for a weekly
TV audience to track meaningfully: **20–35 distinct characters**. Beyond that, the marginal
performer is invisible, regardless of talent. This is the audience-side reason the
oversized roster fails, independent of the financial reason.

## 5. The heat lifecycle

```
   Introduction ──► Curiosity ──► Investment ──► Peak ──► Plateau ──► Decay
        │              │              │            │          │          │
     novelty      "who is this"   "I care"     "I need     "still     "oh, them"
                                              to see it"    good"
        └──────────── 1–6 months ──┴─ 3–18 months ─┴─ 3–12 ──┴── indefinite
```

**The peak is short.** A performer at maximum heat has roughly **3–12 months** at that
level before some decay begins. The booking question is what you cash that peak in for.

### 5.1 The cardinal error
Reaching the peak and **not paying it off**. The audience's willingness to invest is
highest at the peak and declines afterward. A performer who peaks and is not put in the
main event during the peak may never get a second one. Daniel Bryan's 2013–14 arc is
usually cited as the version where the promotion eventually did cash in (at WrestleMania
XXX) after nearly missing the window; the counter-examples — performers who got hot, waited,
and were cold by the time management moved — are far more numerous and less famous,
precisely because nothing came of them.

## 6. The heat economy of a promotion

Heat is **not created from nothing** — it is largely transferred.

- **A star loses to a rising performer:** heat transfers. The star loses a little (they have
  a stock; they can afford it), the riser gains a lot.
- **A star beats a rising performer:** the riser loses a lot, the star gains almost nothing
  (they were already established). **This is a net destruction of value** and is the single
  most common booking waste in the business.
- **Two cold performers wrestle:** no heat is generated. Match quality produces respect, not
  investment.
- **A hot performer is put with a cold one:** some heat transfers down, some is lost.
- **A performer is kept off TV:** heat decays but is not transferred to anyone.

**The promotion-level implication:** total audience investment is roughly conserved in the
short run and grows only through genuinely new investment (new characters connecting, real
stakes, moments). A booker who understands this books to *move* heat toward the people who
can convert it, rather than believing heat can be manufactured on demand.

## 7. Absence as a heat mechanism

One of the most reliable and most under-used tools.

| Absence length | Effect on return |
|---|---|
| **2–4 weeks** | Minor; useful for resetting a stale weekly presence |
| **2–4 months** | Strong. Novelty restored, anticipation built if the absence had a story. |
| **6–12 months** | Very strong return pop; but the character must have somewhere to go |
| **2–5 years** | Nostalgia return; enormous initial reaction that decays within 3–6 appearances |
| **10+ years** | Legend status; a one-night attraction |

**The return premium:** roughly a **3–8 week window** of elevated reaction. What matters is
entirely what happens in that window. A return that leads to nothing burns the asset.

**The corollary nobody applies:** if absence generates heat, then **continuous presence
destroys it**, and a promotion that features its top stars every single week is spending an
asset it could be accumulating. This is one of the clearest structural arguments for
part-time/reduced schedules for top acts — and it is in direct tension with the need to fill
television.

## 8. Diagnosing hot vs cold — the observable signals

| Signal | Hot | Cold |
|---|---|---|
| Reaction to entrance music | Grows before they appear | Polite, or nothing |
| Reaction trend week over week | Rising | Flat or declining |
| Merch | Selling, rising | Flat |
| Crowd signs | Present, homemade | Absent |
| Chants during other segments | Yes | No |
| Social/search interest | Spiking | Flat |
| Reaction to their finisher | Explosive | Applause |
| Crowd stays for their match | Yes | Bathroom break |
| The audience predicts them winning | Yes, and wants it | Doesn't care |

**The single best cheap indicator:** does the crowd react to the *music* before the
performer is visible? That reaction is pure accumulated relationship with no present
stimulus. It is the purest measurement of over-ness that exists.

---

## 9. Can you make someone over?

The honest answer, which the industry has argued about forever:

**You can create the conditions. You cannot force the outcome.**

- The promotion controls: exposure, opponents, wins, presentation, character, time.
- The audience controls: whether they care.
- Promotions can reliably make someone **visible** and **credible**. They cannot make
  someone **beloved**.
- The strongest lever is **pairing them with someone the audience already loves or hates**,
  because it borrows an existing emotional relationship.
- The strongest anti-lever is **visible effort** — an audience that senses it is being
  managed resists.

**The empirical pattern:** roughly, of performers given a genuine sustained main-event push,
maybe a third become genuinely over, a third become accepted without enthusiasm, and a third
are actively rejected. That ratio has been stable for forty years across promotions and
booking styles, which suggests it is a property of audiences rather than of booking skill.

## Sim implications

This is the document with the most direct engine relevance.

- **Separate `Overness` (stock) from `Momentum` (flow).** The current model has
  `Popularity` doing both jobs. Splitting them would immediately enable hot/cold dynamics,
  the peak-and-cash-in decision, and heat decay.
- **Freshness already exists on `Gimmick` (0.0–1.0, decays with use)** — this is the right
  idea and should be extended: freshness per *match-up*, per *stipulation*, per *faction*,
  and per *catchphrase*, with the decay periods in §4.1.
- **Heat transfer on match results** (§6) is the most important missing mechanic. A win over
  a lower-status opponent should be worth almost nothing; a win over a higher-status one
  should transfer meaningfully. The sim currently scores match *quality* but not *status
  economics*.
- **Absence as a mechanic** with a return premium and a decay curve is easy to implement and
  produces immediately recognisable wrestling behaviour.
- **The 20–35 character freshness ceiling** gives the oversized-roster problem a
  *creative* cost as well as a financial one.
- **The peak window** (3–12 months) creates genuine urgency: the game should make it possible
  to miss the window, and it should hurt.
- **The music-pop signal** would be a lovely, thematic UI element: showing the reaction a
  performer gets before they've done anything as the readout of their true over-ness.
