# 06 — Schedule and Cadence

*How many shows, how often, of what kind, at each size of promotion — and why the schedule
is the real skeleton of the business.*

The user question this answers: **"How many shows does the promotion run? How often? How
does that differ based on promotion size?"**

---

## 1. The three kinds of show

Everything a promotion runs is one of three things, and they have completely different
economics and creative rules.

### 1.1 Television (weekly episodic)
- **Purpose:** fulfil a media rights contract; build stories; reach the mass audience
- **Revenue:** rights fee (paid regardless of attendance), plus a modest gate
- **Attendance economics:** irrelevant to profitability at Tier 0/1 — the crowd is set
  dressing for the broadcast. A 60%-full arena on television is a business success and a
  visual problem.
- **Creative rule:** you must produce it whether or not you have anything to say

### 1.2 The premium event (PPV / PLE / "supercard")
- **Purpose:** convert accumulated investment into a purchase
- **Revenue:** historically PPV buys; now overwhelmingly **included in a streaming
  subscription** (WWE Network 2014 → Peacock 2021 → Netflix 2025), which changed the
  economics fundamentally — the event no longer sells itself, it sells *retention*
- **Creative rule:** blow-offs happen here; the build's promises are kept here

### 1.3 The house show / live event (untelevised)
- **Purpose:** pure gate revenue, merch, market maintenance, rep for the roster
- **Revenue:** ticket sales minus building, crew, travel, and talent
- **Creative rule:** **nothing consequential happens.** No title changes (usually), no
  angle advancement, because the TV audience wasn't there to see it. Matches are longer,
  looser, more comedic, and often better *as matches* precisely because there's no camera.
- **Why they exist:** historically they *were* the business (pre-1985, TV existed to sell
  house shows). Now they are a declining supplementary revenue line at Tier 0 and a
  primary one at Tiers 3–5.

## 2. Show counts by tier

### Tier 0 — WWE (mid-2020s, approximate)

| Show type | Cadence | Annual count |
|---|---|---|
| Raw | Weekly, Monday | 52 |
| SmackDown | Weekly, Friday | 52 |
| NXT | Weekly, Tuesday | 52 |
| Premium Live Events | ~monthly + extras | 12–16 |
| Live events (house shows) | Weekends, tour blocks | 100–160 |
| **Total** | | **~270–330** |

**Historical comparison — WWF/WWE at its touring peak (late 1990s–2000s):**
Roughly **350–400 shows a year** across multiple touring units, with performers working
**200–250 dates each**. The reduction since is one of the most significant quality-of-life
changes in the industry, driven by (a) house-show revenue collapsing relative to media
rights, and (b) injury/attrition costs.

**Per-performer schedule at Tier 0 today:** roughly **100–150 dates/year** for a full-time
main-roster performer, concentrated in Mon/Tue TV plus weekend loops. Part-timers and
special attractions may work **6–20**.

### Tier 1 — AEW (approximate)

| Show type | Cadence | Annual count |
|---|---|---|
| Dynamite | Weekly, Wednesday | 52 |
| Collision | Weekly, Saturday | 52 |
| Rampage / secondary | Weekly (varies) | ~40–52 |
| ROH (affiliated) | Streaming, weekly/monthly | varies |
| Pay-per-views | Quarterly + extras | 4–8 |
| House shows | Rare — historically almost none | 0–15 |
| **Total** | | **~110–160** |

**The AEW distinction:** a national promotion that runs **almost no untelevised house
shows**. Its TV tapings *are* its live events. This is a deliberate and unusual choice with
real consequences: lower touring cost and injury load, but no low-stakes venue for
developing performers, no market-maintenance circuit, and no revenue stream independent of
television.

### Tier 2 — NJPW (a very different shape)

NJPW runs **tours**, not weekly TV:
- **~150+ dates a year**, clustered into named tours (New Year Dash, New Beginning, Sakura
  Genesis, Wrestling Dontaku, Dominion, G1 Climax, Destruction, Power Struggle,
  World Tag League, Wrestle Kingdom)
- A tour is a **consecutive run of 8–20 shows in different cities**, mostly small buildings
  (800–3,000), ending in a big-building finale
- **The G1 Climax** alone is ~19–20 shows across a month
- **Wrestle Kingdom** (January 4, Tokyo Dome) is the single anchor event of the year
- Television is a **highlights/streaming** product (NJPW World) rather than a weekly
  episodic narrative engine

This is the fundamental Japanese structural difference: **the tour and the tournament, not
the weekly episode, is the narrative unit.** See
[25-international-models.md](25-international-models.md).

### Tier 2 — CMLL
- **Fixed weekly schedule at fixed venues**, unchanged for decades: Friday night at Arena
  México (the flagship), Tuesday at Arena México, Sunday matinee, Monday at Arena Puebla
- **~200+ shows a year**, almost all in the same handful of buildings
- The most *stable* schedule in professional wrestling

### Tier 3 — Super-indie (PWG, GCW, Beyond, RevPro at scale)

| Show type | Cadence | Annual count |
|---|---|---|
| Regular shows | Monthly-ish | 10–24 |
| Big weekend / tournament | 1–3 per year | 3–9 shows |
| **Total** | | **12–40** |

PWG at its peak ran roughly **8–10 shows a year in one building** and was one of the most
influential promotions in the world. **Show count and influence are not correlated.**

### Tier 4 — Regional independent
- **6–24 shows a year**, typically monthly or every 6 weeks in 2–4 towns
- Often clustered around seasons: spring/fall runs, avoiding summer heat and holiday
  competition
- Frequently tied to a **wrestling school** — students work the shows, tuition subsidises
  the promotion

### Tier 5 — Local
- **2–12 shows a year**, sometimes fewer
- Often single-venue, often a fundraiser or a fair/festival booking

## 3. Why the counts are what they are

### 3.1 The constraint stack
Show count is determined by, in order of hardness:
1. **TV contract obligations** — the hardest constraint; you produce what you sold
2. **Roster size and health** — you cannot run more shows than you have bodies for
3. **Market capacity** — a town can absorb a show every 4–8 weeks before returns collapse
4. **Production cost** — each televised show costs $250k–$1M+ to produce
5. **Creative capacity** — see [05-creative-process.md](05-creative-process.md) §10

### 3.2 The market saturation rule
A given market can support a promotion returning roughly **every 6–12 weeks** at Tier 0/1,
and **every 4–8 weeks** at Tier 3/4 where the audience is smaller but more devoted.
Returning too often produces visible attendance decay:
- 1st visit: strong
- 2nd visit (4 weeks later): −25–40%
- 3rd visit: −50%+

This is the mathematical reason for the **touring loop** (see
[07-live-events-and-touring.md](07-live-events-and-touring.md)) and the reason the
territory system existed at all: a promotion needed 15–25 towns to cycle through so each
one had time to reset.

### 3.3 Why house shows declined
In 1984, house shows were ~70%+ of revenue and TV was a loss-leading advertisement for
them. By 2024, media rights are ~60–70% of revenue and house shows are a low-margin
supplement. The shift happened in stages:
- **1985–1995:** PPV emerges; the supercard becomes the payoff instead of the local blow-off
- **1996–2001:** TV ratings become the competitive battleground
- **2002–2013:** house shows persist at scale but margins thin
- **2014:** WWE Network — direct-to-consumer subscription changes what an event is *for*
- **2019–2025:** rights fees explode; touring shrinks; site fees (Saudi Arabia, and later
  other markets paying for events) become an enormous high-margin line

## 4. The annual calendar

### 4.1 WWE's year (the template most Western promotions echo)

| Period | Event | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **January** | Royal Rumble | Starts the road to WrestleMania; the Rumble winner is the year's first big decision |
| **February** | Elimination Chamber | Second qualifier layer |
| **March/April** | **WrestleMania** | The year's payoff. Two nights since 2020. Everything is built to this. |
| **April** | Draft / shake-up (some years) | Reset the rosters after Mania |
| **May–June** | Backlash, Money in the Bank | MITB briefcase = a year-long story device |
| **July–August** | SummerSlam | The second-biggest show; the summer anchor |
| **Sept–Nov** | Various + Survivor Series (Nov) | The autumn stretch; traditionally the weakest quarter |
| **December** | Year-end event | Reset; set up the Rumble |

**The structural insight:** WrestleMania is not just the biggest show; it is the **fiscal
and creative fulcrum**. The company's year is *"the road to WrestleMania"* and *"after
WrestleMania"*. This produces a predictable creative rhythm — and a predictable
post-Mania slump, when the year's biggest stories have all concluded simultaneously and
nothing has replaced them. **The post-tentpole vacuum is a real, recurring problem** and
the promotions that handle it well (starting new stories *at* the big show, not after it)
outperform.

### 4.2 NJPW's year
| Period | Event |
|---|---|
| **January 4** | **Wrestle Kingdom** (Tokyo Dome) — the year's summit |
| **February** | The New Beginning tour |
| **March** | New Japan Cup (single-elimination tournament → title shot) |
| **April–May** | Sakura Genesis, Wrestling Dontaku |
| **May–June** | Best of the Super Juniors; Dominion (the year's #2 show) |
| **July–August** | **G1 Climax** — a month-long round-robin; the winner gets the Jan 4 main event |
| **Sept–Oct** | Destruction, Royal Quest |
| **Nov–Dec** | World Tag League, Power Struggle → build to Jan 4 |

Note the elegance: **the G1 in August determines the main event of the Tokyo Dome in
January.** The structure generates its own build, five months long, with no angle required.

### 4.3 Seasonality (the real-world constraints)
- **Summer:** good for outdoor/fair dates, bad for arena attendance in many markets
  (competition from vacations, outdoor entertainment)
- **September–November:** historically the weakest wrestling quarter in the US — NFL
  competition, back-to-school, pre-holiday spending caution
- **Late December:** dead for ticket sales, good for TV viewership (people are home)
- **January–April:** the strongest stretch; new year, WrestleMania season, indoor months
- **Holidays:** Thanksgiving (Survivor Series tradition) and the day after Christmas
  (historically huge in the territories and at MSG) are proven dates

## 5. How cadence changes with size — the underlying logic

| Promotion size | Cadence logic |
|---|---|
| **Tier 0** | Cadence is set by **television contracts**. Creative fits the calendar. |
| **Tier 1** | Same, but the promotion has fewer TV hours and more discretion about touring. |
| **Tier 2 (tour model)** | Cadence set by **tour blocks**; intense bursts with recovery gaps. Better for bodies, worse for continuous audience habit. |
| **Tier 3** | Cadence set by **what the core audience will travel for**. Scarcity is an asset — a promotion running 8 shows a year can make each one an event. |
| **Tier 4** | Cadence set by **venue availability and cash flow**. Run when you can afford the deposit and the payoffs. |
| **Tier 5** | Cadence set by **the school's calendar**. |

### 5.1 The scarcity/habit trade-off
This is the key strategic tension in scheduling:

- **High frequency** builds *habit*. Monday night is wrestling night. The audience doesn't
  decide to watch; they just watch. This is enormously valuable and is the foundation of
  Tier 0's business.
- **Low frequency** builds *event feel*. Every show matters. Nothing is filler. This is why
  a PWG show felt bigger than a random Raw despite being 1/50th the size.
- **You cannot have both.** A promotion that runs weekly must accept that most weeks are
  ordinary, and its job is to make the extraordinary weeks legible.

The most common Tier 1 error is **running like Tier 0** (weekly, multiple shows) while
having Tier 3's roster depth and star power — producing a lot of television that
individually feels unimportant.

## 6. Show length

| Show | Length | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WWE Raw | 3 hours (2012–2024), 2 hours since Netflix era in some formats | The three-hour Raw is widely blamed for creative dilution |
| WWE SmackDown | 2 hours | |
| NXT | 2 hours | |
| WWE PLE | 3–4 hours + 1hr pre-show; WrestleMania nights 4+ hours | |
| AEW Dynamite | 2 hours | |
| AEW PPV | 4+ hours — frequently criticised as too long | |
| NJPW tour show | 2.5–3 hours | |
| NJPW Wrestle Kingdom | 5–6 hours | |
| Indie show | 2.5–3.5 hours | The single most common indie mistake is running 4 hours |

**The audience attention curve:** live crowds are strongest in hour 1, fade in hour 2, and
by hour 3 are physically depleted regardless of quality. Television audiences drop steadily
after the first 30 minutes. This is why:
- The hottest angle goes in the **first 15 minutes** (television) or **last 20 minutes**
  (live gate logic) — and the tension between those two is a real strategic choice
- Three-hour shows have a structural quality problem no amount of good booking solves

## 7. Match counts per show

| Show type | Matches | Segments | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2-hour TV | 4–6 | 4–7 | ~50–60 min of actual wrestling after ads |
| 3-hour TV | 6–8 | 6–10 | The extra hour is usually filled with lower-stakes matches |
| PPV/PLE | 6–9 | 2–4 | Longer matches, fewer of them |
| Tentpole (WrestleMania night) | 6–8 | 2–3 | Plus entrances that run 5–10 minutes each |
| House show | 7–9 | 0–2 | Almost pure wrestling |
| Indie show | 7–10 | 1–3 | Often too many |
| NJPW tour show | 7–9 | 0 | Undercard tags building to the top-of-card singles |
| Lucha show | 5–7 | 0–1 | Mostly trios |

**Match length distribution on a typical 3-hour TV show:**
opener 8–12, second 5–8, third 3–6 (or a squash at 1–2), fourth 8–12, main 12–20.
Squash matches (a dominant one-sided win in under 3 minutes) are a deliberate tool for
establishing dominance cheaply and have been under-used in the modern era relative to
their effectiveness.

## 8. Travel and its consequences

The schedule is not just show count — it is the **travel between shows**, which is what
actually destroys people.

- Tier 0 US touring: performers arrange their own flights and rental cars, drive 200–400
  miles between towns overnight, and are responsible for their own accommodation on some
  contracts
- A typical week for a full-timer: fly Sunday, TV Monday, TV Tuesday, fly home Wednesday,
  fly out Friday, two weekend shows, home Sunday night. **Repeat 40+ weeks a year.**
- Historical loads were far worse: 250–300 dates, driving between them, with no offseason.
  This is the root cause of the industry's catastrophic 1980s–2000s mortality and addiction
  history — see [15-injuries-and-attrition.md](15-injuries-and-attrition.md).

**Modern mitigations:** residencies (running multiple weeks in one city), fewer house
shows, chartered travel at the top tier for major tours, and scheduled time off written
into contracts for top talent.

## Sim implications

- **Show cadence should be a strategic choice with real trade-offs**, not a fixed number:
  more shows = more revenue and habit, at the cost of talent fatigue, creative dilution,
  and market saturation.
- **Market saturation** (§3.2) is a clean, satisfying mechanic: each market has a "heat"
  that depletes when you run it and recovers over time. It naturally produces the touring
  loop as an emergent player strategy.
- **The three show types need different rules.** House shows shouldn't advance feuds but
  should generate gate and let you develop workers; TV should build; premium events should
  cash in. The sim currently has one `Show` type — splitting it would immediately create
  strategy.
- **The annual calendar with a fixed tentpole** gives the game its rhythm. The "road to
  the big show" build and the **post-tentpole vacuum** are both good pressure sources.
- **Show length and match count as a budget** already exists (`TotalDurationMinutes`).
  Adding the **attention curve** (diminishing crowd receptivity in hour 3) would make long
  shows a genuine trade rather than just more room.
- **Travel/fatigue accumulation** across a tour is the missing link between schedule and
  the injury/attrition model.
