# 03 — Roles and Competence

*Every job in wrestling: what it does, what "good" looks like, what "bad" looks like, and —
crucially — the **several distinct ways** to be good or bad at each one.*

This is the longest document in the reference because it answers the question that most
often comes up in design: *what does this person actually do, and how do I tell a good one
from a bad one?*

**The organising principle:** almost every role in wrestling has **multiple independent
competence axes**, and people are routinely elite on one and disastrous on another. A
single "skill" number for any of these jobs is the wrong model. The interesting cases —
the ones that make the industry what it is — are the lopsided ones.

---

## 1. THE BOOKER / HEAD OF CREATIVE

**What the job is:** decide what happens, to whom, in what order, and why. Assign wins and
losses, design storylines, build cards, allocate television time, decide who gets elevated
and who gets stalled.

### The competence axes

| Axis | What it measures | Elite example of this axis | Notorious failure of this axis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Long-term architecture** | Can you plan 6–18 months and land it? | Paul Heyman (ECW), Gedo (NJPW 2013–2018), Bill Watts (Mid-South) | The 2000s WWE "write the show Tuesday for Monday" era |
| **Weekly craft** | Can you make a good two hours *this week*? | Bruce Prichard, Michael Hayes on production | Bookers who plan brilliantly but produce dull TV between the big beats |
| **Talent evaluation** | Can you spot who will draw before they draw? | Vince McMahon 1984–1999 (Hogan, Austin, Rock), Heyman | Bookers who push their friends, or who only see what already works |
| **Willingness to change** | Do you pull the trigger when the crowd tells you something? | Turning Austin loose in 1997; the 2015–16 pivot to Roman-alternatives being repeatedly refused is the counter-example | Bookers who "stay the course" through visible audience rejection |
| **Restraint** | Do you know when *not* to do the thing? | Long slow builds; saving the blow-off | Blowing off feuds on free TV; the "every week is the biggest show ever" trap |
| **Fairness/politics** | Do you book the company or your favourites? | — | Booker-wrestlers who beat everyone (see 1.4) |
| **Consistency of world** | Does your fiction hold together? | NJPW's won-loss logic; ECW's coherent aesthetic | Authority figures who forget rules they set two weeks ago |

### 1.1 The ways to be GOOD
There are at least five distinct successful booker profiles, and they do not resemble
each other:

1. **The architect.** Plans in arcs. Every match is a step. Strongest at building new
   stars and at making title reigns mean something. Weakness: can be dull week to week; can
   be slow to react. *(Gedo's Okada build; Watts in Mid-South; Heyman's ECW.)*
2. **The carny.** Instinctive, reactive, sees what's hot and pours gasoline on it. Great at
   capitalising, terrible at planning. *(Classic territory promoters; Jerry Jarrett; parts
   of Vince McMahon.)*
3. **The producer.** Not a great idea generator, but takes other people's ideas and makes
   them land — casting, pacing, presentation, television craft. *(Michael Hayes; Kevin
   Dunn on the production side.)*
4. **The talent whisperer.** Gets performances out of people nobody else could. Builds the
   product around what the roster is genuinely good at rather than a preconceived plan.
   *(Dusty Rhodes as an NXT coach; Shawn Michaels in that same role from ~2019.)*
5. **The systems booker.** Builds structures — tournaments, leagues, ranking systems — that
   generate stories automatically and reduce dependence on the booker's own inspiration.
   *(NJPW's G1/New Japan Cup model; the AEW ranking system in concept.)*

### 1.2 The ways to be BAD
Also at least five, and they fail differently:

1. **The self-booker.** Books themselves strong at the expense of the company. Reduces the
   whole roster to a supporting cast. Kills the promotion's ability to create new stars.
   *(The archetypal territory-killer; see [27-case-studies.md](27-case-studies.md).)*
2. **The overcorrector.** Changes direction every week in response to the last crowd
   reaction. Nothing gets time to develop; the audience learns nothing means anything.
3. **The stubborn.** Has decided who the star is and will not move regardless of evidence.
   Feels "principled" internally; reads as contempt externally. Produces the most toxic
   audience relationship of any failure mode, because the crowd knows it is being
   overruled.
4. **The swerve addict.** Confuses surprise with drama. Every payoff is subverted. Trains
   the audience not to invest, because investment is always punished.
5. **The 50/50 booker.** Protects everyone, elevates no one. Every feud is one win each and
   a coin-flip blow-off. Nobody loses, therefore nobody wins, therefore nobody matters.
   This is the *most common* modern failure and the hardest to see from inside, because
   no individual decision looks wrong.

### 1.3 The subtle failure: booking for the wrong audience
A booker can be technically excellent and still fail by optimising for:
- **The internet audience** when the money is in the live gate and children's merch
- **The live crowd in the building** when the money is in television households
- **Their own taste** (very common with fan-turned-booker)
- **Peers' respect** — "the boys" liking a decision has near-zero correlation with it
  drawing money

### 1.4 The booker-as-wrestler problem
Historically endemic and reliably destructive. When the booker is also an active performer:
- They cannot evaluate their own act
- Nobody can tell them they are cold
- Every other performer's push is competing for the booker's own spot
- Even a scrupulously fair booker-wrestler is *suspected*, which poisons the room

The rare successes (Dusty Rhodes in mid-80s Crockett is the usual cited case, and even that
gets argued about) worked because the person was genuinely the biggest draw at the time.
The failures are innumerable.

### 1.5 "When is a booker's run over?"
Empirically, booker runs decay on a 3–6 year cycle. The mechanism is not that the booker
gets worse; it is that **their instincts become the promotion's conventions and stop being
surprising**. The audience learns the booker's grammar — they know a run-in is coming, they
know the champion doesn't lose in January, they know the returning babyface goes over. The
booker's strengths become predictable, and predictability is the enemy.

## 2. THE OWNER / PROMOTER

**What the job is:** put up the money, take the risk, own the outcome. At Tiers 3–5 this
includes physically running the show.

### Competence axes
- **Capital discipline** — spend on the things that draw, not the things that feel like
  a real wrestling company
- **Market sense** — knowing which towns work and why
- **Delegation** — the hardest one; the modal owner cannot do it
- **Talent retention** — keeping the people who draw, at a price you can pay
- **Deal-making** — TV rights, venues, sponsors, site fees
- **Knowing what you're bad at** — the single strongest predictor of long-run survival

### Ways to be good
1. **The dealmaker** who is mediocre at wrestling but excellent at contracts, television,
   and expansion. Hires a booker and lets them work. *(This is how you get an unspectacular
   product with an unassailable business.)*
2. **The operator** who runs a tight, profitable, unglamorous company for thirty years in
   one region. Never big, never dies.
3. **The visionary** who sees a market shift before anyone else — national expansion in
   1984, PPV, the internet, streaming, a new audience segment.

### Ways to be bad
1. **The mark owner** who buys names instead of building them. Spends the fortune, has a
   spectacular first year, and folds in year three when the money runs out and the names
   are cold.
2. **The meddler** who hires a booker and overrules them weekly. Gets the worst of both:
   no coherent vision and a booker who stops trying.
3. **The absentee** who loses interest. The company drifts, budgets get cut by finance
   people with no product understanding, and it dies quietly.
4. **The one who won't spend.** Underinvests in production and marketing, so a good product
   looks cheap and nobody hears about it.

## 3. THE ROAD AGENT / PRODUCER

**What the job is:** take creative's instruction ("these two go 12 minutes, heel wins with
the belt, set up the rematch") and work with the performers to build the actual match.
Time it, structure it, keep it safe, relay finishes, cue the truck. Then give notes after.

Almost always **former wrestlers**. At WWE there are typically 8–15 producers; one is
assigned to each segment on a show.

### Competence axes
- **Match psychology knowledge** — can they actually structure a match?
- **Communication** — can they explain it to a 22-year-old and to a 45-year-old veteran?
- **Timing/television awareness** — do they know how a match reads on camera, and how to
  hit a commercial break?
- **Safety judgment** — will they stop something dangerous?
- **Credibility** — do the performers respect them enough to listen?

### Ways to be good
- **The teacher** who makes everyone they work with better, permanently
- **The fixer** who can take a match that is dying and rescue it in real time via a
  finish change or a cue
- **The translator** who makes creative's bad idea work anyway

### Ways to be bad
- **The dictator** who lays out every move and produces mechanical, samey matches (a real
  and widely-criticised WWE tendency in the 2010s)
- **The absent** who says "go have a good match" to two people who need help
- **The dinosaur** whose psychology is from 1985 and who kills anything he doesn't
  personally understand
- **The politician** who uses the role to protect friends and bury rivals in their notes

## 4. THE WRITER (Tier 0 only, really)

**What the job is:** script segments, write promos, produce the actual document that is the
show. WWE's writing team is largely drawn from television and comedy rather than wrestling.

### The structural tension
Writers are hired for television craft and are usually **outsiders to wrestling's grammar**.
They produce dialogue that is well-constructed and unspeakable by a wrestler, and stories
that are dramatically sound and psychologically wrong for a live crowd. Meanwhile, the
wrestlers' instinct is right about their own character and wrong about structure. Both
sides know they're right; both are half right.

### Ways to be good
- Writes to the performer's actual voice instead of a generic one
- Understands that a wrestling promo is not dialogue — it is a **direct address to a crowd
  that answers back**
- Protects long-term arcs against weekly churn
- Fights for the roster internally

### Ways to be bad
- **Word-for-word scripting** of people who talk better freely (the single most-cited
  complaint about the WWE writing model)
- **Sitcom-ing** — comedy that undermines threat
- **The revolving door** — writers cycle out every 12–24 months, so nobody carries
  institutional memory, and characters get reinvented by whoever inherits them

## 5. THE PERFORMER (the wrestler)

The role with the most independent competence axes of any in the business. **This is the
one to model most carefully.**

### 5.1 The axes

| Axis | What it is | Someone elite here | Someone weak here |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Drawing power** | Does the audience pay money because of you? | The only axis that pays. | — |
| **Connection / charisma** | Do people care what happens to you? | Austin, Rock, Hogan, Cena, Punk, Bryan | Technically superb workers nobody invests in |
| **Promo ability** | Can you talk? Two sub-skills: **scripted delivery** and **live improvisation** | Rock (both), Heyman (both), Roman post-2020 | Great athletes who cannot cut a promo |
| **In-ring workrate** | Athletic execution, crispness, difficulty | Bryan, Okada, Angle, Ospreay | Limited-move powerhouses |
| **Ring psychology** | Structure, pacing, storytelling, selling logic | Bret Hart, Randy Orton, Jumbo Tsuruta, Bryan | Athletically gifted but sequence-driven performers |
| **Selling** | Making the opponent's offence look devastating | Ricky Morton (the term "Ricky Morton" is *literally* a verb for it), Shawn Michaels, Dolph Ziggler | No-sellers; people who pop up after finishers |
| **Look / presence** | Does the camera and the crowd read you as a star before you do anything? | Lesnar, Rhea Ripley, Vader, Roman | Performers whose presentation reads as mid-card regardless of ability |
| **Character work** | Is there a coherent, watchable person there? | Undertaker, Bray Wyatt, Jericho's reinventions | Performers who are "a wrestler" with a name |
| **Safety** | Do people get hurt working with you? | The most under-discussed axis in the industry | Reputationally career-ending; see 5.4 |
| **Reliability** | Show up, sober, on time, do the finish you were given | — | Ends more careers than any other single factor |
| **Adaptability** | Can you work any style, with anyone, at any length? | Ric Flair's entire reputation is this | Performers who can only have one match |
| **Durability** | Does your body hold up to 150+ dates a year? | — | See [15-injuries-and-attrition.md](15-injuries-and-attrition.md) |

### 5.2 The crucial insight: these axes are only loosely correlated
The industry's most persistent argument is downstream of this fact.

- **Elite worker, no connection.** Great matches, flat crowds, no merch. The most common
  form of "underrated". *(Shayna Baszler and Chad Gable in this repo's roster are explicitly
  built as this archetype.)*
- **Elite connection, limited worker.** Enormous reactions, thin matches, huge merch.
  *(Hogan, Ultimate Warrior, LA Knight; Hulk Hogan drew more money than any technically
  superior wrestler of his generation, several times over.)*
- **Elite talker, no ring.** Can build any feud, cannot deliver a blow-off. Often becomes a
  manager, an authority figure, or a commentator.
- **Elite look, nothing else.** Gets pushed anyway, repeatedly, forever, at every promotion,
  because the look is the thing decision-makers can evaluate instantly.
- **Elite everything, no durability.** The saddest and commonest tragedy.

### 5.3 What actually determines a push
In descending order of real-world weight:
1. **Look and presentation** (unfair, but empirically dominant, especially for a first push)
2. **Who believes in you** (a decision-maker's advocacy)
3. **Crowd reaction that management can't ignore** (the only bottom-up force, and it works)
4. **Merch numbers** (the least deniable evidence)
5. **In-ring ability** (real but the weakest of the five as a *cause* of a push)

### 5.4 Reputation axes that aren't performance
Careers are made and destroyed on these, and they are largely invisible to fans:
- **Safe worker vs unsafe.** A reputation for hurting people means veterans refuse to work
  with you. This is a career death sentence and it is enforced entirely socially.
- **Locker-room citizen.** Helps others, doesn't complain, respects the room.
- **Political operator.** Some of the biggest names in history were feared as much for
  backstage manoeuvring as for drawing.
- **Professional.** Learns the finish, hits the marks, does the media, doesn't no-show.

## 6. THE CHAMPION (as a job, not a prize)

Being champion is a **job with duties**, distinct from being a good wrestler.

**Duties:** work the most dates, do the most media, carry lesser opponents to good matches,
be the reliable focal point, and — the least discussed — **make challengers**.

### Ways to be a good champion
1. **The workhorse.** Works everyone, works often, everyone looks good against them.
   *(Ric Flair as NWA champion is the definitional case: a travelling champion who worked
   ~300 dates a year and made every territory's local hero look like a world beater.)*
2. **The special attraction.** Rarely appears, feels enormous when they do. Only works if
   the promotion has other things to sell in the gaps. *(Lesnar's 2014–2019 reigns.)*
3. **The ace.** The consistent centre of the promotion; the standard others are measured
   against. *(Okada; Tanahashi; Hogan.)*

### Ways to be a bad champion
- **The vacuum.** Never loses, never elevates, so the title's only function is to say who's
  best, which everyone already knows.
- **The absent.** Holds the belt while unavailable, so the top of the card is empty.
- **The unbelievable.** The audience doesn't accept them, so every defence has an
  undercurrent of "why is this person champion?" — and *the audience's disbelief is
  contagious to everything the title touches*.
- **The transitional who wasn't supposed to be.** Someone who was given the belt as a
  stopgap and had to keep it because of injury or a plan change.

## 7. SUPPORT ROLES

### 7.1 The Manager
**What the job is:** talk for someone who can't, add heat, protect a finish, be a
physical target for revenge.
- **Good:** makes the client bigger without eclipsing them; generates hatred; gives
  the babyface someone to want to punch. *(Bobby Heenan, Jim Cornette, Paul Heyman.)*
- **Bad:** becomes the act; the client is a prop. Or adds nothing and is just a person
  standing there.
- **Why the role declined:** WWE largely abandoned managers in the 2000s in favour of
  scripted promos for everyone; the role has been rediscovered periodically because it
  solves the "great worker, can't talk" problem better than any other tool in the business.

### 7.2 The Referee
- **Job:** count, enforce, be the mechanism for DQ/count-out finishes, relay messages,
  check on injuries, and be invisible.
- **Good:** positioning that never blocks the camera or the crowd's sightline; a fast
  count that sells a near-fall; knows when a wrestler is genuinely hurt.
- **Bad:** visible, slow, blocks shots, misses cues, over-acts.
- **The special case:** referees are also a **storytelling device** (slow counts, bumped
  refs, the crooked official). Overused, this destroys the credibility of every finish.

### 7.3 Commentary
Enormously undervalued. The commentary team is **the primary interpreter of everything the
audience sees**, and it can single-handedly make a match feel important or worthless.
- **Roles:** play-by-play (describes, escalates, sells stakes), colour (bias, comedy,
  explanation), and sometimes a third voice.
- **Good:** Jim Ross calling a near-fall makes it a near-fall. Gordon Solie made a
  headlock feel like surgery. Good commentary tells you **who to care about and why**.
- **Bad:** talking about anything other than the match; laughing at the product; failing to
  react to a big moment; being scripted so tightly that a genuine surprise is called flatly.
- **The rule:** *the commentator's job is to make the audience feel what they should feel*.
  When commentary treats a match as filler, the audience does too. This is why a promotion
  with bad announcers has a product that feels smaller than it is.

### 7.4 The Ring Announcer, Timekeeper, Music/Production
Small jobs that are load-bearing. An entrance is 60–70% music and lighting. A blown
entrance cue costs a pop that cannot be recovered.

### 7.5 The Trainer / Coach
- Runs the developmental system, teaches basics, safety, psychology, promo.
- **Good coaches produce identifiable generations.** The Hart Dungeon, Killer Kowalski's
  school, the Dojo systems in Japan (NJPW's Young Lion system), Les Thatcher, Lance Storm,
  the Nightmare Factory, WWE's PC coaching staff.
- **Bad coaching produces a generation of performers who all wrestle the same way** —
  which is a real and recurring criticism of centralised developmental systems.

### 7.6 Talent Relations
- **Good:** honest, fast, keeps promises, tells someone bad news to their face, is trusted
  by both sides.
- **Bad:** avoids people, lets contracts lapse, is seen as purely the company's enforcer,
  gets caught lying.
- **This role's competence determines retention more than money does.** A promotion that
  treats people well loses fewer of them at any given salary.

### 7.7 The Doctor / Athletic Trainer
Under-modelled and consequential. A promotion with real medical staff and real concussion
protocol has different injury outcomes than one that sends people back out. Post-2007 this
became a serious, legally significant function at the top tier.

## 8. Cross-cutting: what "good at your job" means in wrestling

The industry's unifying competence question is: **do you make the company money, and do you
make the people around you better?** Almost everything else is preference. A booker whose
shows are artistically extraordinary and whose houses are empty is failing at the job. A
booker whose shows are formulaic and whose towns are full is succeeding, and the internet
will hate them.

The corollary is the reason wrestling arguments are irresolvable: **quality and drawing are
correlated but not the same thing**, and everyone weighs them differently.

## Sim implications

- **Do not use a single "skill" number for any role.** The multi-axis structure above is the
  point. The interesting characters are lopsided.
- The performer axes in §5.1 map cleanly onto the existing `Wrestler` model but the sim is
  currently missing: **Safety**, **Reliability**, **Adaptability**, **Durability**, and
  **promo-scripted vs promo-improv** as separate values.
- Staff should be **entities with their own multi-axis competence** — producers who improve
  match quality, commentators who modify crowd perception of a match's importance, trainers
  who shape developmental output, talent relations who affect retention.
- **Ways to be bad should be behavioural archetypes, not low numbers.** A "swerve addict"
  AI booker and a "50/50 booker" AI booker both produce bad results in completely different,
  recognisable ways. That is more interesting than `bookingSkill = 34`.
- The booker decay curve (§1.5) is a good long-game mechanic: a booker's *effectiveness*
  should decay as the audience learns their patterns, independent of their stats.
- Commentary quality as a **multiplier on perceived importance** is a cheap, high-value
  addition — it directly modifies how a given match's rating converts into audience response.
