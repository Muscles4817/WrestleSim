# 08 — Television and Media Rights

*The largest revenue line in modern wrestling, how it is won and lost, and how it shapes
everything on screen.*

---

## 1. Why television is the whole game

The central economic fact of modern wrestling:

> **Wrestling's television audience fell roughly 75–80% from its 1999 peak, while
> wrestling's revenue rose several-fold.**

That is not a paradox. It is the result of a structural shift in what television companies
pay for. Understanding it explains nearly every strategic decision made since 2014.

### 1.1 Why rights fees went up while ratings went down
1. **Live, DVR-proof content became scarce and precious.** As audiences shifted to
   on-demand streaming, the only programming that people still watch *live with the ads*
   is sports and sports-adjacent content. Wrestling is 52 weeks a year of live,
   appointment-viewed, ad-watchable programming with no offseason and no player strikes.
2. **Wrestling's audience is unusually loyal and unusually hard to replace.** Networks
   lose the whole audience if they drop it; there's no substitute product.
3. **Streamers entered the bidding.** Peacock, Netflix, Amazon, and others needed
   anchor content with a built-in audience and year-round cadence. Competition among
   buyers raised prices far faster than viewership fell.
4. **Sports rights inflation generally.** Wrestling rode the same wave as every other live
   property.

### 1.2 The consequence for the product
When rights fees dominate revenue:
- **The crowd in the building stops being the customer.** The network is the customer.
- **Ratings and demographic delivery matter more than ticket sales.**
- **Consistency beats spikes.** A network buying 52 weeks wants a reliable floor, not a
  brilliant week and three bad ones.
- **The company's incentive shifts from creating stars to protecting the brand**, because
  the brand is what the network bought. This is arguably the deepest cause of modern
  wrestling's star-creation problems.

## 2. How a rights deal actually works

### 2.1 The structure
- **Term:** typically 3–5 years, sometimes longer
- **Fee:** an annual amount, sometimes escalating year over year
- **Inventory:** exactly how many hours of first-run content, on what nights, at what times
- **Exclusivity:** what the promotion may and may not do elsewhere (this is why a
  promotion's PPVs, library, and international rights are often carved up separately)
- **Production responsibility:** who pays for and controls the broadcast. Wrestling
  promotions almost always **produce their own show** and deliver a finished feed, which is
  unusual and is a large part of why their production departments are so big.
- **Make-goods and guarantees:** occasionally, ratings guarantees with penalties

### 2.2 What the network actually wants
- **Demographic delivery**, specifically adults 18–49 (the "demo") — historically the
  currency of ad sales, and wrestling over-delivers on it relative to total viewers
- **Lead-in and lead-out value** — does the show help the shows around it
- **Predictability** — a stable number they can sell against
- **Brand safety** — content that won't lose advertisers
- **Promotional flexibility** — the ability to cross-promote the network's other content

### 2.3 What the promotion gives up
- **Creative control at the margins.** Standards and practices notes, content restrictions,
  requests for specific talent or crossovers.
- **Scheduling autonomy.** The network decides the night and time slot, and moving a show
  (e.g. WWE moving to different nights, AEW's Rampage shifting time slots) has enormous
  audience consequences the promotion cannot control.
- **Sometimes, the ability to be edgy at all.** The 2008 WWE move to PG was substantially
  about advertisers and a Mattel toy licence, not creative preference.

## 3. The negotiation cycle — the most important 18 months in a promotion's life

**The pattern is extremely consistent:**

```
  T−24 months   Quiet positioning; the promotion starts making the product look healthy
  T−18 months   Serious conversations begin; exclusive negotiating windows open
  T−12 months   Peak "look good" period: returns, big signings, hot angles, stunt
                bookings, anything that spikes ratings and headlines
  T−6 months    Deal usually announced
  T−0           New term begins
  T+3 months    The stunts stop. The product often visibly relaxes.
```

**Practical rule for reading the industry:** if a promotion suddenly starts making
uncharacteristically aggressive moves — big-name signings, surprise returns, cross-promotion,
stadium shows — check when its TV deal expires. This is the most reliable explanatory
variable in modern wrestling.

Documented examples of this rhythm: WWE's 2018 rights cycle (Raw to USA, SmackDown to Fox,
preceded by a talent-signing spree and a run of "biggest ever" claims); AEW's 2023–24
Warner renewal period; NJPW's periodic streaming-partner negotiations.

## 4. Ratings — what the numbers mean

### 4.1 The metrics
| Metric | What it is | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Total viewers (P2+)** | Average audience across the broadcast | Headline number; what fans argue about |
| **18–49 demo rating** | % of adults 18–49 watching | What advertisers historically pay for; **the number that matters** |
| **Quarter-hours** | Audience per 15-minute block | Shows exactly which segments gained and lost viewers — the sharpest creative feedback in existence |
| **Key demo delivery** | Absolute number in the demo | Increasingly used over the rating |
| **Streaming metrics** | Hours watched, completion rate, subscriber acquisition/retention | Opaque; platforms disclose selectively |

### 4.2 The quarter-hour
The single most useful feedback instrument in wrestling. It tells you:
- Which performers **gain** viewers when they appear (a genuine measure of drawing power)
- Which segments cause people to leave
- Whether the audience stays for the main event

Historically, quarter-hour data was leaked/reported weekly and was used internally to make
push decisions. It is also easily over-read: a segment following a hot angle inherits its
audience; a segment at 10:45pm on a Monday loses viewers to bedtime regardless of quality.

### 4.3 The streaming problem
As shows move to streaming (WWE Raw to Netflix in 2025), traditional ratings stop existing.
This has two effects:
1. **Public accountability disappears.** Nobody outside the company knows how a given show
   performed. The weekly ratings argument that has shaped fan discourse since 1995 loses its
   data.
2. **The internal metric changes** from "who watched" to "who subscribed, who stayed, who
   watched to completion". That rewards different creative: less about the hot opening
   segment that holds a live audience, more about being a reason to keep a subscription.

## 5. The pay-per-view → streaming transition

This is one of the two or three biggest structural changes in wrestling history.

### 5.1 The PPV era (1985–2013)
- Buy rate = number of homes purchasing at $30–70
- WrestleMania peaks: ~1.2–1.4M buys (WM23, WM28-era)
- **The entire creative calendar was built around convincing people to buy a specific show
  on a specific night.** Every angle had a purchase attached.
- Monthly PPVs (12/year from the mid-90s) put enormous pressure on creative: a payoff every
  four weeks

### 5.2 The Network era (2014–)
WWE Network launched at $9.99/month with every PPV included. Effects:
- **Instant collapse in per-event revenue**, offset by subscription volume and,
  eventually, by the Peacock (2021) and Netflix (2025) licensing deals that converted
  the D2C business into a rights business again
- **The event stopped being a purchase decision.** You don't buy WrestleMania; you're
  already subscribed. This removed the single clearest measure of whether an angle drew.
- **Creative pressure changed** from "make them buy this show" to "make them not cancel"
- The renaming from PPV to **"Premium Live Event"** is not cosmetic; it reflects that the
  product is no longer sold individually

### 5.3 What was lost
The buy rate was the industry's honest scoreboard. A main event that drew 900,000 buys and
one that drew 300,000 were unambiguously different, and the difference was attributable to
specific performers. **No modern metric replaces it.** This is a real epistemological loss
for the business and is one reason modern arguments about who "draws" are unresolvable.

## 6. Site fees — the other modern revenue innovation

A **site fee** is a flat payment from a host (a government, a tourism authority, a local
promoter, a venue) to bring an event to their location. The promotion takes no gate risk;
the fee is close to pure margin.

- WWE's Saudi Arabia deals (from 2018) are the defining example — reported in the range of
  $40–60M+ per event in some years, transforming WWE's operating margins
- The model has spread: WrestleMania and SummerSlam host cities bid competitively;
  Australia, the UK, and Puerto Rico have all hosted fee-supported events
- **Ethical and reputational cost is real and is part of the calculation** — the Saudi
  events have been a persistent source of controversy, talent discomfort, and press
  criticism

**Why this matters structurally:** a site fee decouples event revenue from event quality
and audience interest. A show can be enormously profitable while being creatively
irrelevant, which changes what the company optimises for.

## 7. Digital, social, and the long tail

- **YouTube** is a genuine revenue line at Tier 0 (billions of annual views, ad share) and
  the primary distribution channel for Tiers 2–4
- **Short-form (TikTok, Reels, Shorts)** drives discovery among audiences who will never
  watch a three-hour show. Wrestling is unusually well-suited to it: the business has
  always produced 30-second emotional payoffs.
- **The library** is an appreciating asset. WWE's archive (WWF + WCW + ECW + AWA + regional
  footage) underpins documentaries, licensing, nostalgia programming, and platform value.
  Acquiring competitors' libraries has been a deliberate long-run strategy.
- **Video games** (WWE 2K, and historically the Fire Pro and Smackdown lines) are a
  licensing revenue line and, more importantly, a major recruitment channel for young fans
- **Podcasts and shoot interviews** are a large parallel economy that the promotions do not
  control and which shapes fan understanding of the business enormously

## 8. How TV shapes what you see on screen

Concrete, causal effects of the television business on creative:

| TV reality | On-screen consequence |
|---|---|
| Hard commercial breaks | Matches structured around rest holds at the break; "picture in picture" formats invented to keep the match visible |
| Hard out time | Shows that end abruptly mid-angle; main events cut short |
| Quarter-hour tracking | Hot opening segment; the biggest star in the first 15 minutes rather than the last |
| Advertiser sensitivity | Blood, language, and violence restrictions; the entire PG era |
| Network wanting stars | Part-timers featured over full-timers; celebrity involvement |
| Multiple shows to fill | Roster spread thin; recap packages; talking segments |
| Global streaming | Less US-market-specific content; more international talent |
| Ratings pressure at a specific time | Stunt bookings timed to sweeps, competition, or negotiations |
| Live vs taped | Spoiler-driven decisions about where to put title changes |

**The single most visible one:** the modern cold open. Wrestling television opens with a
promo or an angle rather than a match because the first quarter-hour sets the number that
gets reported.

## 9. Historical rights landmarks (for calibration)

| Year | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| 1953 | DuMont network airs wrestling | First national US wrestling TV |
| 1972 | Vince McMahon Sr. and the territorial TV syndication model matures | TV as a local advertisement |
| 1984 | WWF national syndication + cable | The territorial system's death warrant |
| 1993 | Monday Night Raw debuts on USA | The weekly live episodic format is born |
| 1995 | WCW Nitro debuts on TNT | Head-to-head competition; the Monday Night Wars |
| 1999–2000 | WWF moves to TNN/Viacom | The first genuinely large rights fee |
| 2001 | WCW cancelled by Turner despite profitability history | **A promotion can be killed by a corporate decision unrelated to wrestling** |
| 2014 | WWE Network launches | D2C streaming; the PPV model ends |
| 2018 | Raw to USA / SmackDown to Fox (~$470M/yr combined, 5 years) | Rights fees explode; the modern era begins |
| 2019 | AEW to TNT | A second national promotion exists again |
| 2021 | WWE Network content to Peacock (~$1B/5yr) | D2C converted back into a rights deal |
| 2023 | WWE + UFC merge into TKO | Wrestling as a portfolio asset in a sports conglomerate |
| 2025 | Raw to Netflix (~$5B/10yr, reported) | Wrestling's largest deal; the streaming era proper |

## Sim implications

- **Media rights should be the dominant revenue line at Tiers 0–2**, with a **contract term
  and expiry date** that the player can see. The negotiation cycle (§3) is one of the
  strongest strategic structures available: a multi-year period where "make the product look
  valuable" is a concrete goal with a concrete payoff.
- **The network as a stakeholder with its own preferences** (ratings, demo, brand safety,
  specific stars) creates non-creative pressure that is genuinely wrestling-authentic.
  Displeasing the network should have consequences: a worse renewal, a time-slot move, a
  content restriction.
- **Quarter-hour ratings** would be an outstanding feedback mechanic — showing the player
  exactly which segments gained and lost audience, with all the misreading potential that
  implies.
- **The PPV → streaming transition** is a good era mechanic: in an early era every event is
  a purchase with a measurable buy rate; in a later era that signal disappears and is
  replaced by murkier retention metrics. Losing your best measurement tool as the game
  progresses is thematically perfect.
- **Site fees** give a lever that trades money against audience relevance — high revenue,
  zero market development, some reputational cost.
- **TV inventory** should have a hard obligation: if you signed for 3 hours a week, you
  produce 3 hours a week whether or not you have material.
