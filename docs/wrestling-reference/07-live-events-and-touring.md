# 07 — Live Events and Touring

*Buildings, routing, markets, attendance, and the physical logistics of putting on shows.*

---

## 1. The venue ladder

| Venue class | Capacity | Who runs there | Typical cost to rent |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bar / club / VFW hall** | 100–300 | Tier 5 | $200–800, or a door split |
| **Armoury / gym / rec centre** | 300–800 | Tier 4 | $500–2,500 |
| **Small theatre / ballroom** | 800–2,000 | Tier 3/4 | $2,000–8,000 |
| **Legendary indie rooms** (2300 Arena Philadelphia, Reseda American Legion, Korakuen Hall Tokyo) | 800–2,000 | Tier 3 | — |
| **Mid arena / civic centre** | 3,000–8,000 | Tier 1/2, Tier 0 house shows | $15,000–50,000 |
| **Major arena** | 10,000–20,000 | Tier 0/1 TV and PPV | $50,000–200,000+ |
| **Stadium** | 40,000–100,000+ | Tier 0 tentpoles only | $500k–several million, plus build-out |

### 1.1 Venue costs are not just rent
The rental figure is the smallest part. Real cost stack:
- **Rent / guarantee vs percentage** — venues often take the greater of a flat fee or a
  percentage of gate
- **Labour** — union stagehands in major markets are a very large and non-negotiable cost;
  load-in and load-out crews, riggers, electricians
- **Insurance** — required, and wrestling is rated as high-risk
- **Security and medical** — mandated staffing ratios
- **Ticketing fees** — the venue's ticketing system takes a cut
- **Concessions and parking** — the venue keeps these, almost always. This is why venues
  will discount rent for a show that draws a big, thirsty crowd.
- **Merch split** — the venue commonly takes **15–35%** of merchandise sold in the building.
  This is a bigger deal than most people realise and is aggressively negotiated.

**Rule of thumb:** a promotion needs to sell roughly **50–60% of a building** to break even
on a straight rental, more in expensive union markets.

### 1.2 The "legendary room" phenomenon
Certain buildings acquire mythic status and *add value to the show held in them*:
Madison Square Garden, Tokyo's Korakuen Hall, Philadelphia's 2300 Arena (ECW Arena),
Arena México, the Hammerstein Ballroom, the Cow Palace, Reseda's American Legion Hall.

The mechanism is real: an audience in a building with history behaves differently — louder,
more participatory, more willing to make the show an event. Promotions pay a premium for
these rooms because the **crowd is part of the product**.

## 2. Routing and the loop

### 2.1 What a loop is
A **loop** is a sequence of towns run on consecutive nights, arranged so travel between
them is short. The classic North American loop: 3–4 towns over a weekend within a few
hundred miles of each other, ending near an airport hub.

Constraints on routing:
- **Drive time** between consecutive towns (300 miles is a normal overnight; 500+ is brutal)
- **Building availability** — arenas have sports teams and concerts; a wrestling date is
  slotted into gaps, often 12–18 months in advance
- **Market spacing** — don't run two nearby towns on the same weekend and split the audience
- **Market recency** — don't return to a town too soon (see [06](06-schedule-and-cadence.md) §3.2)
- **Television anchor** — for a TV promotion, the whole route must return to the TV city on
  the right day

### 2.2 The territorial loop (the historical model)
A territory had a **fixed weekly circuit**: Monday in one town, Tuesday in another, through
the week, repeating. Each town saw the promotion every 7, 14, or 28 days depending on size.
The TV show — usually taped once a week in a studio — advertised the coming loop.

This is the purest form of the business: **television was a 60-minute commercial for the
local live show.** Angles were shot on TV; the payoff was sold in the arena.

### 2.3 The residency model (modern)
Running multiple weeks in one city (WWE's periodic residencies; AEW's repeat visits to
Jacksonville/Daily's Place during 2020–21; NXT's fixed home at Full Sail then the PC).

**Advantages:** massively reduced travel cost and fatigue, a familiar crowd, set-and-forget
production.
**Disadvantages:** the same crowd every week gets jaded and quiet; no market development;
the show can feel small.

## 3. Attendance: what actually drives it

In rough order of impact on ticket sales for a specific date:

1. **Is it a televised/special show or a house show?** (Televised sells 1.5–3× a house show
   in the same market)
2. **Announced main event and card** — an unadvertised card sells far worse
3. **Market history** — a town that has been served well for years turns out; one that got
   burned doesn't
4. **Star presence** — a specific advertised name matters far more at Tier 1–4 than at
   Tier 0
5. **Price** — see §4
6. **Day of week** — Friday/Saturday >> weeknight for house shows; TV date is fixed
7. **Local competition** — a home playoff game will destroy your gate
8. **Marketing spend and lead time** — 6–10 weeks of local promotion is standard; less than
   3 weeks is a visible failure
9. **Weather and season**

### 3.1 The advertised-card problem
Modern television promotions frequently do **not** advertise cards for house shows, or
advertise them and then change them. The audience learns that "Roman Reigns advertised" does
not mean Roman Reigns appears. This is a **slow, compounding destruction of market trust**
and is one of the most-cited reasons for house-show attendance decline. Territories lived
and died on delivering the advertised card.

### 3.2 The papering question
"Papering" a house = giving away tickets to fill seats. Reasons to do it:
- Television optics — an empty building looks like a dying company on camera
- Crowd noise — a full room is louder and the show is better
Reasons not to:
- A papered crowd is a **worse crowd**: they didn't choose to be there, they're less
  invested, and they're quieter than a smaller paid crowd
- It destroys the market's willingness to pay next time

Standard practice: paper the **upper deck / hard camera side** to make the shot look full,
never the expensive seats.

### 3.3 Announced attendance vs actual
Announced figures at every level are unreliable. Promotions inflate; the practice is
ancient and near-universal. Reliable indicators are **gate revenue** (harder to fake, and
sometimes reported by venues or in public filings) and **tickets distributed vs sold**.
Treat any announced attendance number as an upper bound.

## 4. Ticket pricing

### 4.1 The price ladder
A typical arena show has 4–8 price tiers:
- **Ringside / floor front** — the premium; at Tier 0 these are $200–1,000+ for a tentpole
- **Floor** — $80–250
- **Lower bowl** — $50–120
- **Upper bowl** — $25–60
- **Nosebleed / obstructed** — $20–35
- Plus **VIP packages** (meet-and-greet, pre-show access, merchandise bundles) which carry
  extremely high margins and have become a major revenue line since the 2010s

Indie pricing: **$15–40 general admission, $40–75 front row.** VIP/meet-and-greet packages
at $75–200 are often the difference between profit and loss on an indie show.

### 4.2 Dynamic pricing
Since the mid-2010s, majors use dynamic pricing (prices move with demand, like airlines).
This maximises revenue per seat and is widely disliked by the core audience. It also makes
"gate" a less useful comparison across eras.

### 4.3 The gate vs the house
- **House** = number of people
- **Gate** = money collected
These diverge: a 6,000-person house at high prices out-earns a 10,000-person house at low
prices, and promotions increasingly optimise for the former. The strategic consequence is
that **the visible crowd gets smaller while the business gets bigger**, which fans read as
decline and management reads as success. Both are right.

## 5. Merchandise at live events

Live merch is disproportionately important:
- **Per-head spend:** $3–8 at a Tier 0 arena show; $8–20 at a Tier 3 indie (the indie
  audience buys much more per person because they're the hardcore)
- **Venue split:** 15–35% off the top in arenas; usually 0% at an indie you rent outright
- **Talent split:** varies wildly. Tier 0 typically pays a royalty percentage on the
  performer's own merchandise. Indies often let the wrestler keep 100% of their own table
  sales — which is frequently a bigger payday than the booking fee.
- **The gimmick table** is a genuine social institution at the indie level: the performer
  sits with their merch, meets fans, sells photos. It builds the direct relationship that
  is the entire basis of independent-scene economics.

## 6. The economics of a single show, worked

### 6.1 A Tier 4 indie show (500-seat room)
```
  REVENUE
    Tickets:  280 paid × $22 avg          $6,160
    VIP:       20 × $70                   $1,400
    Merch (promotion's own)                 $700
    Concessions/door merch cut              $300
                                        ─────────
                                          $8,560
  COSTS
    Venue rental                          $1,500
    Insurance                               $400
    Ring transport + crew                   $500
    Referee, timekeeper, ring announcer     $300
    Talent (14 wrestlers, $75–400 ea.)    $2,600
    Named guest booking                   $1,500
    Advertising / posters / social           $400
    Streaming production (2 cameras)        $600
                                        ─────────
                                          $7,800
  NET                                       $760
```
This is a *good* night for a Tier 4 promotion. The margin is thin, the variance is high,
and one rained-out night or one no-show headliner erases three months of profit.

### 6.2 A Tier 0 house show (8,000-seat arena)
```
  REVENUE
    Tickets:  5,400 paid × $58 avg      $313,000
    Merch (net of venue split)           $22,000
                                       ──────────
                                        $335,000
  COSTS
    Venue + labour + security            $85,000
    Ring crew, transport, production     $30,000
    Talent payoffs (30+ performers)     $120,000
    Travel and accommodation              $45,000
    Local marketing                       $15,000
                                       ──────────
                                        $295,000
  NET                                    $40,000
```
Note how thin this is. **House-show margins at Tier 0 are genuinely poor**, which is
precisely why the touring schedule shrank once media rights money arrived. The show exists
now for market presence, roster reps, and merch as much as for profit.

### 6.3 A televised show
The gate is a rounding error against the **rights fee**, which is paid whether 3,000 or
15,000 people attend. Production cost for a weekly TV episode is roughly **$250k–$1M+**
depending on tier and scale. The show is profitable because of the contract, not the crowd.

## 7. Market strategy

### 7.1 Market classification
Promotions think about towns in categories:
- **A markets** — big cities, big buildings, reliable. New York, Chicago, LA, Toronto,
  Philadelphia, Dallas, Mexico City, Tokyo, London. Used for PPVs and tentpoles.
- **B markets** — solid mid-size, dependable house-show towns
- **C markets** — small, occasional, good for filling a routing gap
- **Hot markets** — regionally over-performing for cultural reasons (the Northeast and the
  Carolinas for wrestling historically; Chicago and Philadelphia for hardcore crowds;
  Osaka for Japanese wrestling)
- **Dead markets** — burned by over-running, a bad show, or a promotion that folded owing
  people money

### 7.2 The most valuable thing a promotion owns in a market
**Habit.** A town that has had wrestling on the third Saturday of the month for fifteen
years has an audience that doesn't need to be persuaded. Building that takes years;
destroying it takes about three bad shows.

### 7.3 International touring
- **High-margin when done as a site-fee deal** (a local promoter or government pays a flat
  fee to host; the promotion takes no gate risk). Saudi Arabia's deals with WWE from 2018
  are the extreme version and were transformative for WWE's margins.
- **High-risk when done on gate** — unfamiliar market, unpredictable costs, currency and
  logistics risk
- **Very high value for brand development**: UK, Japan, Mexico, Australia, Germany, India
  and Saudi Arabia have all been major targets for the same reason — existing wrestling
  culture plus underserved demand.

## 8. Physical logistics — the parts nobody thinks about

- **The ring travels separately** and must arrive 6–10 hours before doors. A ring truck
  breaking down cancels a show.
- **Ring setup** takes a trained crew 60–120 minutes; teardown 45–60.
- **Ropes and canvas** are consumables. The mat gets replaced; blood and sweat mean
  cleaning between shows.
- **The production truck** for a televised show requires venue power, a parking position
  with cable runs, and satellite/fibre uplink booked in advance.
- **Pyro** requires permits, a licensed operator, and fire-marshal approval per venue —
  and is denied in some buildings.
- **Lighting rigs** need rigging points; older buildings can't hold modern rigs, which
  quietly disqualifies a lot of venues from televised use.
- **Medical** — at minimum an ambulance on site and a doctor or paramedic. In many
  jurisdictions this is legally mandated for a combat-sports-adjacent event.
- **Athletic commission** — some US states and Mexico require licensing, event permits,
  and sometimes drug testing. WWE's 1989 testimony in New Jersey that wrestling was
  predetermined entertainment was primarily a **regulatory and tax manoeuvre** to escape
  athletic-commission oversight, not a creative decision.

## Sim implications

- **Markets as persistent entities** with heat, history, size, and price sensitivity is
  probably the single highest-value non-creative system to add. It makes touring a real
  decision.
- **The market decay/recovery curve** creates the loop as an emergent strategy.
- **Advertised card vs delivered card** as a trust variable is an elegant mechanic: pull a
  star from an advertised house show to save them for TV, gain short-term flexibility, lose
  long-term market trust.
- **Venue selection** (size, cost, prestige, merch split) turns each show into a small
  economic decision, and the "legendary room" bonus is a nice touch — a crowd multiplier
  attached to a building.
- The **worked economics** in §6 are directly usable as the arithmetic model for show
  profit/loss at each tier.
