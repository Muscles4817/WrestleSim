# 23 — Fanbase Segments

*Who actually watches wrestling, what each group wants, how they conflict, and why booking
for one alienates another.*

---

## 1. Why segmentation matters

Wrestling's audience is not one audience. It is several audiences with **incompatible
preferences** watching the same show, and almost every fan complaint in existence is really
a complaint that the product is being made for a different segment.

A booker who does not know which segment they are serving will produce a product that
serves none of them. A booker who serves only one will cap their business at that segment's
size.

---

## 2. The segments

### 2.1 The casual / lapsed-adjacent viewer
**Size:** the largest potential group by far. Most of the people who have ever watched
wrestling are in this category.

**What they want:**
- Recognisable stars they've heard of
- Simple, legible conflicts
- Spectacle and big moments
- No homework required

**What they don't want:**
- Continuity requirements
- Characters they don't know
- Long matches
- Anything requiring knowledge of what happened three months ago

**How they're reached:** tentpole events, celebrity involvement, viral moments, cultural
crossover. **They are the difference between a good business and a phenomenon** — every
boom period in wrestling history came from reaching this group.

**How they're lost:** the moment the show requires knowledge they don't have.

### 2.2 The regular viewer
**Size:** the core television audience. Watches weekly, follows the stories.

**What they want:**
- Stories that progress and pay off
- Consistency
- Characters they've grown attached to
- A reason to watch every week

**What they don't want:**
- Stories that go nowhere
- Being told to care about someone new every month
- Their favourites being buried

**This is the audience the weekly product is actually made for**, and the one whose habit is
the promotion's most valuable asset.

### 2.3 The hardcore / "smart" fan
**Size:** small in absolute numbers, enormous in influence — they generate the discourse,
buy the most merchandise per head, travel to shows, and set the narrative that reaches
everyone else.

**What they want:**
- Match quality
- Long-term booking coherence
- Their favourites (usually workrate-based) elevated
- To be respected as intelligent
- Novelty and dream matches

**What they don't want:**
- Formula
- Being condescended to
- Athletically limited performers in main events
- Nostalgia acts taking spots from full-timers

**The trap:** hardcore fans are the loudest, the most present in the building, and the most
visible online. They are therefore systematically over-weighted in a booker's perception of
"what the audience wants" — and they are not the largest revenue source. **Booking for the
internet is one of the most reliable ways to shrink a business.**

**The counter-trap:** hardcore fans are also right more often than promotions admit,
particularly about who is genuinely over, because they pay closer attention.

### 2.4 Children and families
**Size:** significant, and disproportionately valuable — a child fan is a 40-year customer,
and families buy multiple tickets and enormous merchandise.

**What they want:**
- Clear good and evil
- Colourful, larger-than-life characters
- Merchandise they can wear
- Heroes who win

**What they don't want / cannot be shown:** graphic violence, adult content, moral ambiguity

**The strategic tension:** the family audience is why PG content ratings exist at Tier 0,
and the PG era is the single most contentious content decision in modern wrestling. The
business case is real: toy licensing, advertiser access, family ticket sales, and a
generational pipeline. The creative cost is also real: reduced stakes, reduced realism,
constrained storytelling.

### 2.5 The lapsed fan
**Size:** enormous — vastly more people have stopped watching wrestling than currently watch.

**What brings them back:**
- A returning name they remember
- A cultural moment big enough to break containment
- A nostalgia hook
- Curiosity about a change

**What sends them away again:** discovering they don't know anyone, discovering it's the
same as when they left, discovering it's nothing like when they left.

**This is the audience every "we're back" marketing push targets** and the hardest to
convert, because their memory is of the product at their personal peak of engagement, which
no current product can match.

### 2.6 The regional/national loyalist
Fans of a specific promotion because it is *theirs* — a local territory audience, a Japanese
puroresu audience, CMLL's Arena México regulars. Enormously loyal, generationally
transmitted, and largely indifferent to what other promotions do.

### 2.7 The wrestler-specific fan
Follows a person, not a promotion. Will follow them to another company. A growing segment in
the social-media era, and the reason performer-owned brand value has increased relative to
promotion-owned brand value.

### 2.8 The ironic / meta fan
Watches partly for the absurdity, participates in hijacking, treats the show as a
communal joke. Real, present in significant numbers at certain shows, and a genuine
booking hazard — they will actively work against a serious moment.

## 3. The conflicts between segments

| Segment A wants | Segment B wants | Conflict |
|---|---|---|
| Casual: recognisable stars | Hardcore: fresh talent elevated | Part-timers vs full-timers |
| Family: clear heroes | Hardcore: moral complexity | Tone and content rating |
| Hardcore: long, athletic matches | Casual: short, punchy segments | Pacing and match length |
| Regular: continuity | Casual: no homework | How much story a show assumes |
| Hardcore: workrate | Casual: charisma and spectacle | Who gets pushed |
| Lapsed: nostalgia | Regular: their current favourites | Screen time allocation |
| Wrestler-fan: their guy featured | Everyone: a balanced show | Booking priority |

**No booking decision satisfies all of these.** This is why every wrestling show has a
segment of the audience complaining, permanently, forever, regardless of quality.

---

## 4. The strategic question: who do you book for?

| Strategy | Ceiling | Floor | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Book for casuals** | Highest — this is where booms come from | Loses the core if you never reward attention | Alienating the loyal audience that funds you between booms |
| **Book for regulars** | Solid, sustainable | Never breaks out | Gradual decline as the audience ages without replacement |
| **Book for hardcores** | Low — the segment is small | High loyalty | Critical acclaim with a shrinking business |
| **Book for families** | High, generational | Constrained creatively | Losing the 18–34 audience that drives cultural relevance |
| **Book for nobody in particular** | — | — | The actual most common outcome |

### 4.1 The correct answer
**Book for the regulars, with a legible surface for casuals and enough craft to keep the
hardcores from actively campaigning against you.**

Concretely:
- Stories should reward continuity but be understandable without it
- The main event should feature people a casual viewer would recognise
- Match quality should be high enough to satisfy the core
- The product should be safe enough not to lose the family audience or the advertisers

Every successful promotion in history has done a version of this. The boom periods happened
when a product built this way produced something that broke containment and reached
casuals at scale.

## 5. Demographics

*Approximate, based on published audience data over the years.*

| Dimension | Typical distribution |
|---|---|
| **Gender** | Historically ~65–70% male; the gap narrowed substantially from the 2010s as the women's division was invested in |
| **Age** | Median audience age has risen steadily; a persistent industry anxiety. Wrestling's audience aged with it rather than being replaced. |
| **Household income** | Skews below median — a factor in ticket pricing sensitivity |
| **Geography (US)** | Over-indexes in the South, the Northeast, and the Midwest; under-indexes on the West Coast |
| **Ethnicity** | Over-indexes among Hispanic and Black audiences relative to general television, which is why Latino and multi-ethnic star power has outsized commercial value |
| **International** | Growing share; streaming made international audiences directly monetisable for the first time |

### 5.1 The aging audience problem
The most serious long-term structural issue in wrestling. The audience that grew up in the
1980s and 1990s booms largely stayed; the replacement rate has not matched. Consequences:
- Nostalgia acts have disproportionate drawing power (which reinforces the problem)
- The product skews toward references the existing audience understands
- New-fan acquisition requires reaching people through channels the existing product
  doesn't use (short-form video, games, cultural crossover)

**Every promotion knows this and none has solved it.** The interventions that work —
children's programming, toys, games, celebrity crossover, social-first content — all take a
decade to pay off, which is longer than most decision-makers' tenure.

---

## 6. How fans consume wrestling now

The behaviour has changed fundamentally and this affects booking:
- **Very few people watch a three-hour show live in full.** They watch clips, highlights,
  and the segments that matter.
- **Results are known instantly.** Surprise is nearly impossible to preserve.
- **Discourse is continuous.** Fans discuss a show for a week; the discussion is part of
  the product.
- **The "shoot" content ecosystem** — podcasts, interviews, dirt sheets — means fans know
  the backstage story, which changes how they read the on-screen story.
- **Clip-first consumption** rewards self-contained moments over long-form storytelling.

**The strategic implication:** a modern wrestling show is simultaneously a three-hour
broadcast and a supply of 30-second clips, and those two products have different optimal
structures.

## 7. Fan behaviour to model

| Behaviour | Trigger |
|---|---|
| **Adopting a performer** | Authenticity + being underused + a moment |
| **Rejecting a push** | Sensing the promotion is overruling them |
| **Hijacking a show** | Boredom + a self-aware crowd + a show that has lost them |
| **Chanting for someone absent** | Wanting something the promotion isn't providing |
| **Lapsing** | A broken promise, a departed favourite, or simply life |
| **Returning** | A cultural moment, a returning name, a friend's recommendation |
| **Buying merchandise** | Identification with a character, not admiration of a wrestler |
| **Travelling to a show** | Event feel, a specific match, a community |

## Sim implications

- **Multiple audience segments with separate satisfaction values** is one of the strongest
  available additions. A show should produce different scores for casual, regular, hardcore,
  and family audiences, and those scores should drive different revenue lines: casual →
  tentpole gate and ratings; regular → weekly ratings and habit; hardcore → merch, indie
  crossover, discourse; family → merchandise and licensing.
- **Segment conflict as the core tension:** the same booking decision raises one segment and
  lowers another. This turns booking from optimisation into genuine strategy.
- **Segment composition varies by market and show type** — a hardcore market crowd rewards
  different things, which ties directly into the crowd-psychology model.
- **The aging audience** as a slow, background variable that the player must actively invest
  against, with interventions that pay off years later, is a superb long-game mechanic and
  authentically models the industry's real problem.
- **Fan adoption/rejection as an autonomous audience behaviour** — the audience picking
  someone the player wasn't pushing — would be the single most wrestling-authentic thing the
  sim could do.
