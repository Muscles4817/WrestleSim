# 14 — The Locker Room

*Backstage culture, the informal hierarchy, politics, and why the social system affects the
product as much as the booking does.*

---

## 1. Why this matters

Wrestling's backstage culture is not gossip; it is a **functioning informal institution**
that governs safety, quality, and career outcomes. It exists because:
- Performers must physically trust each other with their health, nightly
- The work requires cooperation between people who are competing for the same spots
- Management cannot supervise what happens in a ring in real time
- The business historically operated in secrecy, which made internal norms the only
  enforcement mechanism

The result is a strong, unwritten code enforced socially rather than contractually.

---

## 2. The informal hierarchy

Distinct from the card hierarchy, and only partly overlapping:

| Tier | Who | Basis of standing |
|---|---|---|
| **The top guy(s)** | The biggest draw | Drawing power; often has management's ear |
| **Locker-room leaders** | Respected veterans, not always top of the card | Longevity, reliability, willingness to help |
| **Agents/producers** | Former wrestlers now management | Formal authority plus veteran standing |
| **The established mid-card** | Long-tenured regulars | Tenure and dependability |
| **The rising** | New talent with a push | Momentum, but no standing yet |
| **The new** | Recent arrivals | None. Expected to be quiet, helpful, and useful. |
| **The outsiders** | Celebrity crossovers, non-wrestling hires | Tolerated, watched carefully |

**Standing is earned by:** paying dues, working safely, doing jobs without complaint,
helping younger talent, not politicking visibly, being reliable, and having drawn money.

**Standing is lost by:** hurting people, refusing to lose, complaining publicly, going over
people's heads, no-showing, and — most reliably — being seen to have gotten a spot
politically rather than earning it.

---

## 3. The unwritten rules

Some are safety, some are hierarchy, some are superstition. The durable ones:

1. **Protect your opponent.** Their health is your responsibility. This is the actual
   first rule of the business.
2. **Do the finish you were given.** Changing it without agreement is close to unforgivable.
3. **Don't expose the business unnecessarily.** Weaker now than in the kayfabe era, but a
   version survives — you don't undercut the fiction gratuitously.
4. **Respect the veterans.** Say hello, don't take liberties, don't outshine them without
   permission on their own match.
5. **Don't go over people's heads.** Talk to your producer, not the owner.
6. **Don't stiff people** (hit them harder than agreed) unless the match calls for it and
   both agreed.
7. **Don't steal spots or moves** — particularly a finisher.
8. **Be on time, be sober, be ready.**
9. **Help the young talent.** The most respected veterans are the ones who do.
10. **What happens in the locker room stays there.** Weakening rapidly in the podcast era.

### 3.1 The enforcement mechanism
Historically, **physical** — the "receipt" (hitting back harder), the ritualised
"locker-room court", or being deliberately stretched (out-wrestled painfully) by a veteran
in a match. This is much reduced but not extinct.

The modern enforcement is **social and economic**: veterans decline to work with you, your
producer's notes get harsher, your name stops appearing in pitches, and management hears
about it. **Being unsafe or unprofessional is career-ending through refusal, not
through firing.**

## 4. Politics — what it actually means

"Backstage politics" is a real phenomenon with specific mechanisms, not a vague grievance.

### 4.1 The mechanisms
| Mechanism | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Proximity** | Travelling with, training with, or socialising with a decision-maker. Enormously powerful. |
| **The plane/car** | Historically, the top guys travelled with management and the booking got done in transit. |
| **The pitch** | Being in the room when creative is decided |
| **The veto** | Having enough standing to say "I don't want to work with them" or "that doesn't work for my character" |
| **The relayed opinion** | Telling a decision-maker that someone else is unreliable/unsafe/not ready |
| **The protected finish** | Negotiating not to lose, or not to lose cleanly |
| **The alliance** | Groups of performers with shared interests, formal (a faction that exists backstage too) or informal |

### 4.2 Why it is inevitable
- Resources are scarce and zero-sum
- Decisions are made subjectively by a small number of people
- Performance quality is genuinely hard to measure
- The people making decisions are personally close to some of the people affected

**Any organisation with those four properties has politics.** Wrestling is not unusual in
kind, only in the degree to which decisions are personal and the absence of formal process.

### 4.3 The famous cases (for calibration, not gossip)
- **The Kliq (1996)** — a group of top WWF performers with substantial influence over
  booking, and the Curtain Call incident where they broke kayfabe on camera. Notable
  because it demonstrated a bloc of talent overriding the promotion's fiction, and because
  the fallout was distributed unequally.
- **WCW's creative control era** — contracts that let performers refuse losses; see
  [11](11-contracts-and-talent-money.md) §4. The clearest case of politics becoming a
  structural, business-destroying problem.
- **The Montreal Screwjob (1997)** — a promotion changing a finish without telling the
  performer. The most consequential single backstage event in modern wrestling: it created
  the Mr. McMahon character, arguably launched the Attitude Era, and permanently changed
  what performers assume about trust.

## 5. Morale — what drives it and what it affects

### 5.1 What raises morale
- **Being used.** Screen time and a direction. The number one factor.
- **Honesty** about plans, even bad news
- **Fairness** — merit-based decisions, or at least visibly consistent ones
- **Money**, but less than outsiders assume
- **Good travel and schedule**
- **A leader who protects the room** — a top guy who advocates for the roster
- **Winning** — a promotion doing well is a happier place regardless of individual position

### 5.2 What destroys it
- **Broken promises about creative.** Cited more than anything else in post-career accounts.
- **Arbitrary decisions** with no explanation
- **Visible favouritism**, especially toward someone the room considers undeserving
- **Fear of cuts**
- **Outsiders taking spots** (celebrities, other-sport converts pushed past the roster)
- **Being told nothing**
- **Injuries handled badly**

### 5.3 What morale actually affects
- **Effort** — a demotivated performer works safe and unremarkable matches
- **Risk-taking** — both creative and physical; a scared roster is a boring roster
- **Retention** — departures cluster among the people with options, which is exactly the
  wrong group to lose
- **Recruitment** — the industry is small and everyone talks
- **Safety** — an unhappy, distracted room has more accidents

---

## 6. Veterans as a resource

The most underrated asset a promotion has is a small number of respected veterans who:
- Teach the young talent
- Set the room's standard for professionalism
- Can be trusted to have a good match with anyone, including someone green
- Absorb losses without complaint and make them mean something
- Give management honest feedback the roster won't give directly

**A promotion without veterans has to invent its culture from scratch every few years.**
This is a real, observable difference between promotions that have been running for
decades and new ones — and it's one reason new promotions hire experienced performers into
non-featured roles.

Conversely, **veterans who won't move on** are a distinct problem: taking spots, refusing
to lose, and enforcing a style of wrestling the audience has moved past.

---

## 7. The culture shifts

Backstage culture has changed substantially and modelling any era requires knowing which
version applies:

| Era | Culture |
|---|---|
| **Territory (pre-1985)** | Absolute kayfabe. Faces and heels travelled separately and did not socialise publicly. Extreme hazing. Physical enforcement. Cash payoffs. |
| **National expansion (1985–1996)** | Kayfabe erodes; steroid and drug culture at its peak; the road schedule at its most brutal |
| **Attitude (1997–2001)** | Kayfabe substantially abandoned; enormous money; the "boys' club" culture at its most excessive; devastating mortality rates in this cohort |
| **Post-Benoit (2007–)** | Wellness policy, concussion protocol, drug testing. Culture changes fast and permanently. Sobriety becomes normal rather than exceptional. |
| **Modern (2015–)** | Professionalised. Performers are athletes with trainers and nutritionists. Social media means nothing stays backstage. Podcasts have made the business's internal history public. Mental health is discussable. |

**The modern locker room is dramatically healthier and dramatically less private than any
previous version.** Both facts have consequences: safer careers, and a culture in which
internal disputes become public content within days.

---

## 8. Factions backstage vs on screen

On-screen factions often reflect real backstage groups (travel partners, training partners,
friends) because it is easier to book people who already have chemistry. This is generally
good — real relationships read as real on camera.

The failure mode is when a backstage bloc becomes powerful enough to influence booking in
its own favour, at which point the on-screen faction is a symptom of an organisational
problem.

---

## Sim implications

- **Locker-room standing as a value separate from card position** creates authentic
  dynamics: a mid-card veteran with high standing who can be trusted with green talent is a
  genuinely valuable asset the audience never sees.
- **Morale as a multi-factor value** (usage, honesty, fairness, money, schedule) that
  affects effort, injury rate, retention, and departure requests.
- **Politics as a mechanic** rather than a mood: performers with high standing can veto
  finishes, request opponents, or lobby against others. The player deciding whether to
  accommodate is a real choice with real costs.
- **Veterans as trainers**: a "makes others better" attribute that raises the development
  rate of young talent sharing a locker room and a card with them.
- **Trust as a promotion-wide value**: broken creative promises and mishandled releases
  should reduce it, and low trust should raise contract demands and departure risk across
  the roster.
- **Era-dependent culture** modifies injury rates, substance risk, retention, and how
  quickly information leaks.
