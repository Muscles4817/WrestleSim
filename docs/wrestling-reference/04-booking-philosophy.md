# 04 — Booking Philosophy

*The craft of deciding what happens. Schools of thought, structural principles, the
recurring arguments, and the failure modes that recur across seventy years.*

---

## 1. What booking is actually optimising

A booker is trying to maximise, over time:

```
  REVENUE  =  (people who care)  ×  (how much they care)  ×  (how many ways they can pay)
```

Broken into the levers a booker actually controls:

1. **Create investment** — make the audience care about specific people
2. **Escalate it** — raise stakes over time so that caring becomes urgent
3. **Convert it** — put the payoff behind a ticket, a subscription, a merch item, a TV tune-in
4. **Renew it** — after the payoff, rebuild, or the audience has no reason to come back

Every booking principle below is a heuristic for one of those four. The tension in the job
is that **converting** (cashing in) always costs some **investment**, and renewing takes
time the schedule may not give you.

### 1.1 The fundamental trade: heat is a depleting resource
Heat is built slowly and spent quickly. A feud built over four months and blown off in one
match has converted a stock of investment into a single payday. The booker's core skill is
**pacing the spend**:
- Spend too slowly → the audience gets bored, heat decays on its own, and you get less than
  you built
- Spend too quickly → you get one big night and an empty pipeline
- Spend at the wrong time → you cash a WrestleMania-sized payoff on a random Tuesday

This is the single most important concept in booking and it is the concept most often
violated by promotions under weekly-television pressure.

## 2. The schools

### 2.1 Territorial / Southern booking (Mid-South, Memphis, Crockett, Watts, Jarrett)
**Premises:** Wrestling is a con on a rotating local audience. Build to a house-show payoff.
The babyface suffers, the heel cheats, and the audience pays to see it made right.

**Characteristics:**
- Long, patient babyface-in-peril builds; heels get **months** of dominance
- The blow-off is a **live gate**, sold in a specific building on a specific night
- Finishes are protected: DQ, count-out, and screwjob finishes are used to keep both people
  strong and sell the rematch
- Territory logic: because the audience turned over and you only ran a town every 3–4 weeks,
  you could run the same angle across a circuit
- **Heat comes from injustice.** The whole system runs on making the crowd angry at
  unfairness and then paying it off.

**Why it worked:** the fiction was protected (kayfabe), the audience was local and loyal,
and the payoff was always a ticket purchase.

**Why it stopped working:** national television destroyed the geography that made it
possible, and an audience that knows it's a work responds differently to injustice.

**What survives:** babyface-in-peril structure, the long build, the value of a protected
finish. Every good modern feud still uses this skeleton.

### 2.2 The sports-presentation school (NJPW, AJPW, NWA at its most serious, Lou Thesz-era)
**Premises:** Present it as a sport. Won-loss records matter. Rankings matter. Titles are
earned. Character is secondary to competition.

**Characteristics:**
- **Tournaments and leagues as the story engine** (G1 Climax, Champion Carnival, Best of
  the Super Juniors). Round-robin formats generate meaningful matches automatically,
  produce natural contenders, and create stakes without requiring an angle.
- Long matches, clean finishes, minimal interference
- Career arcs: young lion → excursion → return as a new man → contender → ace
- A **very clear internal hierarchy** the audience can read

**Strengths:** it scales without a genius booker; the structure produces stories. Stakes are
legible. Great matches are the product, and great matches are repeatable.
**Weaknesses:** limited emotional range; hard to create a phenomenon; poor at recruiting
casual audiences who don't want a sport.

### 2.3 The Attitude / crash-TV school (WWF 1997–2001, WCW Nitro, ECW)
**Premises:** Television is the product. Every segment must hook. Never let the audience
change the channel.

**Characteristics:**
- Fast pacing, cold opens, cliffhangers, unadvertised returns
- Angles > matches; matches often exist to set up angles
- Edgier content, real-life blurring, "reality" as a device
- Weekly big moments — free-TV title changes, huge surprises

**Strengths:** it produced the highest-rated wrestling in history and pulled in an audience
that had never watched wrestling.
**Weaknesses:** it is **a treadmill that accelerates**. Each week must top the last, which
consumes ideas at an unsustainable rate. WCW's collapse and WWF's post-2001 malaise are
both partly the hangover from this. **Crash TV eats years of story in months.**

### 2.4 The sports-entertainment / brand school (WWE 2002–present)
**Premises:** The company is the star. Talent are cast members. The brand outlives everyone.

**Characteristics:**
- Long-term investment in *company-owned* IP over performer-owned charisma
- Scripted promos, consistent presentation, production polish
- Tentpole events on a fixed annual calendar; the "road to WrestleMania" as the master arc
- Cautious, insulated booking — protect the brand, avoid dependency on any one person

**Strengths:** extremely durable; survives departures, injuries, scandals. Maximises the
value of media rights, which is where the money is.
**Weaknesses:** systematically suppresses the organic star-making that created the brand's
value in the first place. It is a strategy that harvests without planting, and it works
for a very long time before it doesn't.

### 2.5 The workrate / alternative school (ECW, ROH, PWG, early AEW, Dragon Gate)
**Premises:** The match is the product. Serve the fans who care most.
**Characteristics:** Match quality as the primary metric, dream matches, high-difficulty
work, respect for the audience's intelligence, cross-promotional openness.
**Strengths:** intense loyalty, strong word-of-mouth, critical acclaim, a genuinely
differentiated product.
**Weaknesses:** the ceiling is the size of the hardcore audience. Escalating athleticism
has a **physical cost curve** and a **diminishing returns curve** — when every match is a
five-star epic, none of them are, and the roster gets injured.

### 2.6 Lucha libre
**Premises:** Spectacle, masks, trios, ritual. The mask is identity; losing it is a career
event.
**Characteristics:** Trios as the default format; three-fall matches (2/3 falls); a
técnico/rudo axis that is more about presentation than morality; *apuestas* (mask vs mask,
hair vs hair) as the ultimate stipulation, with legal/commission weight.
**Why it matters even outside Mexico:** it is proof that a completely different structural
grammar can sustain a mass audience for ninety years.

## 3. Core structural principles (broadly agreed across schools)

### 3.1 Make the audience want something and delay giving it to them
The engine of all drama. In wrestling specifically: establish the desire (the heel must be
beaten), obstruct it (repeatedly, credibly), then pay it off (once, decisively, for money).

### 3.2 Wins and losses must mean something, or nothing does
The currency of wrestling is the win. If wins are distributed at random — the 50/50 problem
— the currency inflates to worthlessness and no amount of match quality restores it.
The corollary: **you cannot make someone by protecting them; you make someone by having
them beat people the audience believes in.**

### 3.3 Somebody has to lose
A booker's hardest job is convincing valuable people to do the job. Every "nobody loses
clean" booking pattern comes from a booker who can't or won't have that conversation.

### 3.4 Elevate through proximity, then through victory
Standard star-making sequence:
1. Put them on screen with someone the audience already cares about
2. Have them survive / hang / get a moral victory
3. Have them win by fluke or by help
4. Have them win clean
5. Have them win clean in a big spot, on a big show, with the whole company watching

Skipping steps produces the "why is this person here?" reaction. Doing all five takes
6–18 months. Almost every genuinely made star followed this shape.

### 3.5 Protect the finish
The last thing the audience sees is the thing they remember. A bad finish retroactively
damages a great match; a great finish can rescue a mediocre one. Finish structure is the
highest-leverage decision in a match.

### 3.6 The 3-match feud shape
The classic and still-dominant structure:
- **Match 1:** Heel wins, usually with help/cheating. Establishes the injustice.
- **Match 2:** Babyface wins, but non-decisively (DQ, count-out, or a win with an asterisk),
  or the heel escapes. Escalates.
- **Match 3:** Blow-off, decisive, in a stipulation that removes the escape route
  (cage, no-DQ, last man standing). The babyface wins, or the heel wins to set up a
  bigger revenge arc.

Variants abound but the **escalation + removal of escape routes** is the load-bearing part.
See [20-storylines-and-feuds.md](20-storylines-and-feuds.md).

### 3.7 Every show needs a reason to exist
Weekly TV without a distinct hook becomes wallpaper. The best-performing weekly shows
always have: an announced main event, an announced consequence, or an announced arrival.
"Tune in and see" beats "tune in".

### 3.8 The card as a composition
A show is not a list of matches; it is a **shaped experience**:
- **Opener:** hot, short, gets the crowd loud. Never your best match — you want to *raise*
  them, not peak them.
- **Cool-down after a hot spot:** the audience physically cannot sustain 3 hours of peak.
- **Contrast:** don't run two similar matches back to back (this is why the sim's
  `same-kind fatigue ×0.85` is correct).
- **The workrate match:** somewhere in the middle-upper card, given time.
- **The main event:** the payoff, the most invested pairing, the highest stakes. It does not
  have to be the best match; it has to be the one they came for.
- **The send-home:** the audience should leave happy, or leave furious and buy a ticket for
  the rematch. Never leave them indifferent.

## 4. The recurring arguments

These are unresolved on purpose — they are genuine trade-offs, not errors.

| Argument | Side A | Side B | Reality |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Long-term planning vs reactivity** | Plans land bigger | The crowd tells you the answer | Both: plan a destination, stay flexible on the route |
| **Protect the stars vs make new ones** | Established names draw now | You need the next one | A promotion that never does the second dies in ten years |
| **Match quality vs storytelling** | Great matches are the product | Nobody buys a ticket for a workrate match with no story | Story sells the ticket; quality sells the *next* ticket |
| **Kayfabe vs reality** | Protecting the fiction creates investment | The audience knows; using reality creates the biggest angles | Reality-based angles are the most potent and most dangerous tool available |
| **Give the fans what they want vs what they need** | The audience is the customer | Audiences ask for the thing that would ruin the story | They are right about *who* they like and wrong about *when* to pay it off |
| **Big man vs small man** | Presence draws, "you can't teach size" | The audience picks who they pick | Look accelerates a push; connection sustains it |

### 4.1 "Give them what they want, but not when they want it"
The most-quoted booking maxim and the most-abused. It's correct about **timing** and
becomes catastrophic when used as an excuse to never deliver at all. The audience will
wait; they will not wait forever, and once they conclude you are never going to pay it off,
they stop asking — which looks like the heat "dying" but is actually the audience leaving.

**The empirical rule:** the point of no return is roughly **when the crowd's chants change
from enthusiastic to sarcastic**. Before that, delay builds. After that, delay destroys.

### 4.2 Booking by committee
Consistently worse than a single voice, for a specific reason: committees produce
**compromise**, and compromise in wrestling means nobody's idea is executed with
conviction. The result is 50/50 booking as an emergent property. Committees are also
unable to take the risks that create stars, because the risky option always has someone in
the room against it.

The functional version is: **one decider, several dissenters who are actually listened to.**

## 5. The booker's toolkit — the actual moves

| Tool | What it does | Cost of overuse |
|---|---|---|
| **The turn** | Resets a character, generates enormous heat | Cheap turns destroy alignment as a concept |
| **The debut/return** | Instant attention, a free pop | If the follow-up is nothing, you burn the surprise for nothing |
| **The title change** | Signals a new era, transfers status | Frequent changes devalue the belt |
| **The stipulation** | Escalates, removes escape routes | Every match becoming a gimmick match makes the cage mean nothing |
| **The injury angle** | Writes someone off, creates a revenge motive | Audience cynicism about real vs worked injuries |
| **The screwjob finish** | Protects both, sells the rematch | Fans feel cheated; three in a row and they stop paying |
| **The faction** | Elevates several people at once, creates numbers-game drama | Factions bloat; every faction eventually splits and that's the story |
| **The tournament** | Creates stakes with no angle required | Predictable; fills TV but doesn't create investment on its own |
| **The authority figure** | Explains why matches happen; a heat magnet | Becomes the show; the wrestlers become subordinate characters |
| **The open challenge** | Elevates whoever answers; creates surprise | Only as good as the answer |
| **The interference/run-in** | Protects a loser, escalates a feud | The single most overused tool in wrestling; it makes finishes worthless |
| **The promo segment** | Builds character, explains stakes cheaply | Talking segments are cheap TV, which is exactly why they get overused |

### 5.1 On interference specifically
Run-ins are the cheapest way to do three things at once (protect the loser, escalate, keep
a story going). This is why every promotion overuses them and why every promotion's fans
complain about them. **The cost is invisible per-instance and enormous in aggregate:** an
audience that has seen ten run-ins in a month stops believing any pinfall will happen, and
therefore stops reacting to near-falls, which destroys the primary drama mechanism in
wrestling.

## 6. Booking for different objectives

The right booking is different depending on what the company needs *right now*:

| Objective | Booking posture |
|---|---|
| **Sell tickets in a specific town** | Announce a match, in that town, with a local hook, and deliver it |
| **Grow television ratings** | Cliffhangers, advertised segments, a reason to be there at 9:00 and at 10:45 |
| **Sell a pay-per-view** | 4–6 weeks of concentrated build; the card must be legible 3 weeks out |
| **Build long-term stars** | Slow elevation, protected wins, screen time with established names |
| **Survive a TV negotiation** | Everything looks maximally healthy: hot angles, returning names, strong numbers |
| **Cut costs** | Fewer talents on screen, more talking segments, longer matches, reduced touring |
| **Reset after a disaster** | Simplify. One clear babyface, one clear heel, one clear stake |

The mistake is applying the wrong posture — e.g. booking for critical acclaim when the
business needs house-show gates, or booking for surprise when the business needs
reliability.

## 7. The classic failure sequence

Promotions fail creatively in a recognisable order:

1. **Something works.** A star gets over, a format hits, ratings climb.
2. **The formula gets codified.** It's repeated because it works.
3. **The audience learns the formula.** Reactions soften. Novelty is gone.
4. **Management increases the dosage** rather than changing the formula. More swerves,
   more title changes, more run-ins, more shocking moments.
5. **Diminishing returns become negative returns.** The audience is now being asked to
   react harder to things that mean less.
6. **Management blames the talent.** New signings, roster churn, "nobody's stepping up".
7. **The audience with alternatives leaves; the audience with none stays out of habit.**
   Business is now running on inertia — see [28-cultural-inertia.md](28-cultural-inertia.md).
8. Either a **reset** (a new booker, a genuine crisis, an outside competitor) or a slow
   decline to irrelevance.

This sequence has run in WWF/WWE (1993, 2004–2008, 2018–2021), WCW (1998–2001), ECW
(1999–2001), TNA (2009–2013), and NJPW (2005–2011, then again post-2020). It is the
industry's most reliable pattern.

## Sim implications

- **Booking schools should be selectable postures with real trade-offs**, not flavour text.
  A "sports presentation" promotion should get structural benefits (tournaments generate
  interest without angles) and structural costs (harder to create phenomena).
- **Heat as a depleting resource that must be spent** is already the sim's core loop
  (`FeudBook` accumulates heat, blow-offs spend it). The missing half is the *cost of not
  spending it*: heat should decay, and a feud left running too long past its peak should
  actively lose value, not merely stop gaining.
- The **crowd's sarcastic-chant threshold** (§4.1) is a great modellable signal: a
  measurable point at which delay flips from positive to negative.
- **Interference overuse** should have a **promotion-level** counter, not just a per-match
  one: every run-in reduces the credibility of all near-falls company-wide for some
  decay period. That is the real mechanism and it would create interesting pressure.
- The **five-step star-making sequence** (§3.4) is an excellent basis for a push system —
  skipping steps should produce measurable audience rejection.
- **Card composition** rules (opener/cooldown/contrast/main) are partly in `ShowSimulator`
  already (position multipliers, same-kind fatigue). Worth extending to a full "shape of
  the show" score.
