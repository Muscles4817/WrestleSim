# 05 — The Creative Process

*How a week of wrestling television actually gets made — from long-term plan to the moment
someone walks through the curtain.*

---

## 1. The planning horizons

A functioning promotion works on four nested timescales simultaneously.

### 1.1 The year (12 months out)
Decided by business, not creative:
- Tentpole event dates and cities (booked 12–24 months ahead, with venue deposits)
- Tour routing and building holds
- Television commitments (number of episodes, specials, network requests)
- Major contract expirations

**Creative's year-level decisions:** who is the champion at the biggest show, and who wins.
At WWE the classic version is *"who main-events WrestleMania and who goes over"* — the
single decision from which the previous nine months are reverse-engineered.

### 1.2 The quarter / the build (2–4 months out)
- The main-event picture for the next tentpole
- Which feuds run through it
- Title changes and their placement
- Returns and debuts timed for maximum effect
- Which two or three people are being **elevated** this cycle

This is the level where booking actually happens. Below it is execution; above it is
scheduling.

### 1.3 The month / the PPV build (4–6 weeks)
The build to a major event is the industry's fundamental narrative unit:

| Week | Function |
|---|---|
| **W-6 / W-5** | Establish the conflict. The reason the match must happen. |
| **W-4** | Announce/sign the match. Now it exists as a purchasable object. |
| **W-3** | Escalate. Physical confrontation, a cost, a complication. |
| **W-2** | Peak the heat. The most intense moment of the build. |
| **W-1** | Contain and go home. Face-to-face, contract signing, final words. **Do not do the big brawl here** — the audience must still want to see it. |
| **Event** | Deliver. |

The most common error: peaking in week 1 and having nothing to escalate to.
The second most common: **giving away the match on free TV during the build**, which
removes the reason to buy.

### 1.4 The week (the episode)
Now it's television production. See §3.

## 2. Where ideas come from

In descending order of frequency at a major:

1. **The long-term plan** — the destination that was set months ago
2. **The decision-maker's instinct** — "I want to see X and Y"
3. **The writers' room** — pitched, workshopped, approved
4. **Talent pitches** — a performer proposes something for their own character
5. **Crowd reaction** — the audience elevates someone and creative follows (rare, powerful)
6. **Necessity** — an injury, a no-show, a departure forces a rewrite
7. **Sponsor/network requests** — a network wants a special, a sponsor wants an integration
8. **Copying** — the competition did something that worked

### 2.1 Talent pitching
How it works in practice: a performer requests time with a writer or the head of creative,
usually at a television taping, and pitches a direction. Realistic hit rate is low. What
makes a pitch succeed:
- It's **specific** and short
- It **makes money legibly** (a match people would pay for, a merch idea, a moment)
- It doesn't require anyone else to lose status
- It is pitched to the person who can approve it, not to someone who will relay it

What makes a pitch fail: asking for a push, criticising current creative, requiring a
significant reallocation of TV time, pitching to the wrong person.

## 3. The week of television — the actual production

### 3.1 The document
The show exists as a **format sheet / rundown**: a segment-by-segment breakdown with times,
which evolves from a rough outline into a minute-by-minute document. Typical structure:

```
  SEG  TIME   ITEM                                       NOTES
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  1    8:00   COLD OPEN — champion promo                 5:00  ends w/ interruption
  2    8:05   MATCH: A vs B                              8:00  A over, clean
       8:13   COMMERCIAL                                 2:30
  3    8:16   BACKSTAGE — segment                        2:00
  4    8:18   MATCH: tag                                10:00  finish: run-in
       ...
  N    9:50   MAIN EVENT                                12:00  DQ finish, brawl to close
       9:58   ANGLE / GO-HOME                            2:00
```

Two constraints dominate: **hard commercial breaks** (fixed times sold to advertisers) and
**hard out** (the show must end exactly on time — going long means being cut off mid-angle,
which has happened memorably and disastrously).

### 3.2 The timeline of a TV day

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Previous days** | Ring crew and production trucks travel and arrive first |
| **6:00–8:00 am** | Trucks in, load-in begins, ring built, set assembled |
| **9:00 am–1:00 pm** | Production setup, lighting, camera blocking, rehearsals for complex spots |
| **~11:00 am–2:00 pm** | Talent arrives; some travel same-day |
| **Early afternoon** | **The rundown is distributed.** Many performers learn what they're doing *today* |
| **Afternoon** | Producers meet with talent, matches are laid out, promos rehearsed or scripted |
| **~4:00–5:00 pm** | Production meeting; commentary meeting; final rundown revisions |
| **~6:00 pm** | Doors open; dark match / warm-up match to get the crowd going |
| **8:00 pm** | Live |
| **During show** | Times get cut and added live; producers relay changes via agents and referees |
| **After** | Post-show notes, dark-match main event to send the crowd home happy, load-out |
| **Overnight** | Trucks to the next city |

### 3.3 The changes
Rewrites on the day are normal at every promotion and were pathological at WWE for two
decades. Causes: a performer is unavailable, a segment runs long, the decision-maker
watched a rehearsal and hated it, a crowd reaction in hour one changes hour two, network
gives a note.

**How changes get communicated live:** through producers/agents at the curtain, via
referees (an earpiece and a hand signal), and through the "gorilla position" — the area
directly behind the entrance curtain, named after Gorilla Monsoon, where the head of
production sits with monitors, timing, and the authority to change anything.

**Gorilla position is the actual command centre of a wrestling show.** Anything that
happens live is decided there.

### 3.4 The commentary layer
Commentary receives the rundown with notes on what to emphasise. At WWE, commentary has
historically been heavily directed through earpieces during the show — the producer feeds
lines and priorities. This is why WWE commentary sounds uniform and why a genuinely
surprising moment sometimes gets called flatly: the announcer is being talked to.

## 4. The taping model vs the live model

| | **Live** | **Taped** |
|---|---|---|
| Spoilers | None | Results leak within minutes; the internet has them |
| Reactivity | Can change based on tonight's crowd | Locked |
| Cost | Higher — a truck and crew every week | Much lower — tape 3–6 weeks in one or two nights |
| Crowd | Fresh every show | Same crowd for 6 hours; they get exhausted and quiet, which sounds bad on TV |
| Risk | Anything can go wrong on air | Can fix in edit |

**The taping trade-off is the single biggest production-economics decision below Tier 0.**
Most Tier 1–3 promotions tape in blocks. WWE runs Raw and SmackDown live and tapes NXT.
The crowd-exhaustion problem is real and audible: the fourth hour of a taping is always
the flattest, which is why smart tapings put the best material in hours one and two and
tape the "third week" episode's opening while the crowd is still fresh (out of order).

## 5. Scripting: the spectrum

| Model | Where | What talent gets | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Full script** | WWE, 2000s–2010s | Word-for-word promos, memorised | Consistent, controllable, frequently stilted; homogenises voices |
| **Bullet points** | WWE at its better moments, AEW for veterans | "Hit these three beats, get to this line" | Retains voice, keeps structure |
| **Direction only** | Territories, Japan, most indies, AEW broadly | "You're angry about the title, go 3 minutes" | Best performers shine, weakest flounder |
| **Nothing** | Old-school | "You're on" | Sink or swim |

**The core trade-off:** scripting raises the floor and lowers the ceiling. A promotion with
25 people who can't talk benefits from scripting; a promotion with 5 elite talkers is
destroyed by it. The industry's most famous promos are almost all unscripted or
substantially self-written.

### 5.1 The finish
Separate from the promo script and treated far more seriously. Finishes are:
- Decided by creative, communicated by a producer
- Sometimes withheld until immediately before the match (to prevent leaks, and historically
  to prevent politicking)
- Rehearsed, especially anything involving interference, weapons, or timing

**Changing a finish after it's been given** is a serious act. Doing it without telling one
participant is the mechanism of a **screwjob** (Montreal, 1997) and is close to the worst
thing a promotion can do to a performer's trust.

## 6. Match layout — how a match actually gets built

The performers and their producer meet, usually a few hours before. What gets decided:

1. **The finish** — always first; everything works backwards from it
2. **The shape** — who controls, when, for how long; where the comeback is; how many
   near-falls
3. **The time** — a hard number, with a signal system for "5 minutes left" / "go home"
4. **The spots** — the specific set pieces, especially anything dangerous
5. **The story** — what this match is *about*, in one sentence
6. **Cues** — how the referee will communicate time, how commercial breaks are handled

The amount of pre-planning varies enormously by style and generation. Two experienced
workers may agree only on a finish and a time and **call it in the ring** — communicating
during the match through holds and clinches. Modern high-spot styles require much more
choreography because the spots are too complex to call live safely.

**"Calling it in the ring" is the mark of high-level trust and skill**, and the industry
has drifted away from it as matches have become more athletically complex and more
televisually precise.

## 7. Feedback loops — how the company knows if it worked

| Signal | Speed | Reliability | What it actually measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The live crowd** | Instant | High for *who*, low for *what* | Who this specific audience cares about |
| **Quarter-hour ratings** | Next day | High | Whether people stayed or left during a segment |
| **Total viewership** | Next day | Medium | Aggregate interest, heavily affected by lead-in and competition |
| **Merch by performer** | Weekly/monthly | Very high | Willingness to actually spend money on a person |
| **Ticket sales by market** | Continuous | Very high | The real business |
| **Social engagement** | Instant | Low–medium | Attention, not investment; easily gamed |
| **Streaming/VOD replays** | Days | Medium–high | What people rewatch |
| **Reaction from "the boys"** | Instant | Very low | Peer taste, near-zero correlation with money |

**The most important and most misread signal:** a loud crowd reaction tells you the audience
cares about a *person*, not that they approve of the *booking*. Boos for a babyface mean
they care and disagree — which is far more valuable than silence, and is routinely
misdiagnosed as failure.

**Silence is the actual failure state.** Not boos. See
[16-crowd-psychology.md](16-crowd-psychology.md).

## 8. Contingency — what happens when it goes wrong

Every promotion has to handle these constantly:

| Problem | Standard response |
|---|---|
| **Injury mid-match** | Referee signals (crossed arms / X), producer calls an audible, finish moved up |
| **Injury before show** | Rewrite the segment; substitute; write the injury into the story |
| **Talent no-show** | Fill with a longer match, a promo, or a pre-taped segment |
| **Segment runs long** | Cut time from a later match — always a match, rarely a promo |
| **Show runs short** | Extend the main event or add an angle |
| **A big story collapses** (departure, scandal, legal) | Pivot; the promotion's ability to do this fast is a real competency |
| **Crowd is dead** | Producers change the shape live — more comebacks, faster finishes |
| **Crowd hijacks the show** (chanting for something else) | Either ignore it, incorporate it, or lose the segment |

## 9. What "good process" looks like

- A **destination** that is decided and known to a small group
- **Weekly plans distributed early enough** for performers to prepare
- **Fewer, better decision-makers** rather than a large approval chain
- Someone with **explicit authority to say the show is done being changed**
- **Post-show review** with real metrics, not vibes
- **Talent told the truth** about where they stand

## 10. What "bad process" looks like — and its symptoms on screen

| Process failure | What the audience sees |
|---|---|
| Same-day rewrites | Characters behaving inconsistently week to week |
| No long-term plan | Stories that stop rather than end |
| Writers churn | Someone's character resets every 6 months |
| Approval chain too long | Nothing edgy, nothing surprising, everything safe |
| Decision-maker overrides everyone | One person's taste visible in every segment |
| No feedback loop | The same thing failing repeatedly with more resources thrown at it |
| Too much TV to fill | Talking segments, recap packages, and matches with no build |

That last one deserves emphasis: **the amount of television a promotion produces is a
creative constraint disguised as a business decision.** Five hours a week of first-run
programming requires roughly 250 hours of content a year. There are not 250 hours of good
ideas. Expanding TV inventory without expanding the creative capacity to fill it is one of
the most reliable ways to degrade a product, and it has happened at WWE (three hours of
Raw from 2012), WCW (Nitro to three hours in 1998), and AEW (Dynamite + Rampage +
Collision + Ring of Honor).

## Sim implications

- The **build calendar** (§1.3) is a strong candidate for an explicit game structure:
  weeks-until-event as a first-class variable that gates what booking actions are
  appropriate, with penalties for peaking early or giving away the match.
- **TV inventory as a creative constraint** is an excellent strategic mechanic: more TV =
  more revenue and more exposure, but the same creative capacity spread thinner, with
  quality decay. This makes "should we add a show?" a genuinely hard decision.
- **The rundown/format sheet** is essentially what `Show`/`ShowSimulator` already is. Adding
  commercial breaks, a hard out, and time-cut pressure would make show assembly feel like
  the real job.
- **Feedback signal quality** (§7) is a great information-design mechanic: give the player
  several signals of differing reliability, and let them misread the unreliable ones.
  In particular, distinguishing "boos because they care" from "silence" should be modelled
  explicitly — the sim currently has crowd energy but not crowd *disagreement*.
- **Contingency events** (injuries, no-shows, overruns) turn show assembly into an active
  problem rather than a static plan.
