# 15 — Injuries and Attrition

*The physical cost of the business: injury rates, the aging curve, career length, and the
historical mortality problem.*

---

## 1. The fundamental fact

Professional wrestling is **choreographed but not fake**. The impacts are real, the falls
are real, and the volume is enormous: a full-time performer at the top tier takes several
hundred bumps a week, 40+ weeks a year, for a decade or more, with no offseason.

The result is an injury and attrition profile closer to an NFL career than to any other
entertainment profession — but spread over 15–20 years instead of 3–4, with no team
doctors, no guaranteed contracts (historically), and no players' union.

---

## 2. Injury types and their frequency

### 2.1 Acute injuries (the visible ones)

| Injury | Frequency | Typical time out |
|---|---|---|
| **Concussion** | Very common; historically undiagnosed | Days to months; cumulative damage is the real issue |
| **Knee** (ACL/MCL/meniscus) | Common | 6–12 months for ACL |
| **Shoulder** (labrum, rotator cuff, separation) | Very common | 4–9 months |
| **Neck** (disc herniation, fusion) | Career-defining | 6–18 months; often career-ending |
| **Back** (disc, facet, fracture) | Extremely common | Weeks to career-ending |
| **Ankle/foot** | Common | 2–8 weeks |
| **Elbow / biceps tear** | Common | 4–6 months |
| **Pectoral tear** | Common in powerlifting-built performers | 4–6 months |
| **Broken bones** (orbital, jaw, ribs, hands) | Occasional | 3–12 weeks |
| **Achilles** | Occasional, devastating | 9–12 months |
| **Catastrophic spinal** | Rare | Career-ending or worse |

### 2.2 Chronic conditions (the ones that end careers)
- **Degenerative disc disease** — near-universal among long-career performers
- **Arthritis** — hips, knees, shoulders
- **Cumulative concussion effects / CTE** — the industry's most serious long-term issue
- **Chronic pain** — which historically drove the painkiller dependency problem

### 2.3 The neck
Wrestling's signature career-ender. Neck injuries are common because of:
- Bumping flat on the back with the head whipping
- Powerbombs, piledrivers, suplexes taken hundreds of times
- Landing on the head in botched or high-risk spots

The industry's most famous neck cases — Steve Austin (1997 piledriver, effectively ending
his in-ring career by 2003 at 38), Edge (2011 forced retirement, later cleared and
returned), Bret Hart, Kurt Angle, Daniel Bryan (2016 retirement, later cleared and
returned), Paige — define the risk profile. **A neck injury at 30 can end a career that
would otherwise have run to 45.**

## 3. Injury rate — approximate modelling numbers

*These are estimates synthesised from injury reporting and roster-absence patterns, not
published epidemiology. Treat as design coefficients, not facts.*

| Metric | Approximate value |
|---|---|
| Probability a full-time performer misses time to injury in a given year | **35–55%** |
| Probability of a significant injury (3+ months) in a given year | **10–18%** |
| Average missed time per performer per year | **6–12 weeks** |
| Percentage of a Tier 0 roster injured at any given moment | **10–20%** |
| Probability a career is ended by injury rather than choice | **~40–50%** |
| Concussions per full-time performer per year (historically, mostly undiagnosed) | **1–3** |

### 3.1 Risk multipliers
| Factor | Effect on injury risk |
|---|---|
| **Match volume** | Roughly linear. 200 dates is ~2× the risk of 100. |
| **Style** — high-flying, hardcore, high-impact | 1.5–2.5× |
| **Age over 35** | 1.5–2× and rising sharply |
| **Existing injury history** | Strongest single predictor. A prior back or neck injury roughly doubles recurrence risk. |
| **Size** (very large performers) | Higher — more force, more joint load, worse landing mechanics |
| **Conditioning** | Poor conditioning sharply increases late-match injury risk |
| **Working with an unsafe opponent** | Large and real |
| **Ring quality** | A hard or badly-maintained ring is a genuine injury multiplier; indie rings are frequently much worse |
| **Fatigue / travel** | Significant, poorly quantified |

## 4. The aging curve

Wrestling has an unusual career arc because two of its three core competencies *improve*
with age while the third declines.

```
   Peak
    │                    ┌──────────────┐
    │        Character  ╱                ╲
    │        + Promo   ╱                  ╲___
    │                 ╱                        ╲
    │      ┌─────────╱                          ╲
    │     ╱   Ring psychology                    ╲
    │    ╱                                        ╲
    │   ╱                                          ╲
    │  ╱    Athleticism ╲                           ╲
    │ ╱                  ╲___                        ╲
    └────────────────────────────────────────────────────
     20      25      30      35      40      45      50
```

| Age band | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| **18–23** | Physically capable, no idea what they're doing. Highest injury rate from inexperience. |
| **24–28** | Athletic peak. Learning psychology. The "great matches, thin character" window. |
| **29–35** | **The prime.** Athleticism still high, psychology developed, character formed. Almost every all-time great's best work is in this band. |
| **36–42** | Athleticism declining, psychology and character at peak. Can still be the top draw — drawing power often peaks here. Match style must adapt or the body breaks. |
| **43–50** | Special attraction territory. Reduced schedule, shorter matches, protected booking. Value is name and character. |
| **50+** | Rare full-time. Occasional matches, authority figures, legends appearances. |

### 4.1 The critical insight
**Drawing power peaks later than athletic ability.** Hogan's biggest years, Austin's,
Rock's, Flair's, Cena's, Roman's — all came at or after the athletic peak. This is because
drawing power is a function of **accumulated audience relationship**, which takes a decade
to build.

This creates the industry's core scheduling tension: performers are most valuable at the
point their bodies are least able to sustain the schedule.

### 4.2 Style-adjusted longevity
- **Powerhouse/brawler styles** age relatively well (less repeated high-impact bumping,
  offence based on presence)
- **Technical/mat styles** age extremely well (Flair, Regal, Finlay all worked well past 40)
- **High-flying styles** age badly — the offence itself is the damage
- **Hardcore/deathmatch styles** age catastrophically. Mick Foley's career is the canonical
  cautionary account.

## 5. Career length

| Stage | Typical duration |
|---|---|
| **Training** | 6 months–2 years before the first match |
| **Indie grind** | 3–7 years before any meaningful opportunity |
| **Peak earning window** | 8–15 years, if it happens at all |
| **Full career** | 15–25 years for those who last |
| **Age at first Tier 0 signing** | Usually 22–30 |
| **Age at retirement** | 35–45 typical; 50+ for those who transition to reduced schedules |

**The attrition funnel** is brutal: of everyone who starts training, perhaps 5–10% will
have a sustained professional career at any level, and well under 1% will reach a national
promotion.

## 6. Injury management — how promotions handle it

### 6.1 The modern protocol (post-2007, Tier 0)
- Ringside physician at every event
- Mandatory concussion evaluation (ImPACT baseline testing)
- Mandatory clearance before return
- Company-paid treatment and rehabilitation for in-ring injuries
- Contract tolling during absence
- A dedicated medical and physical therapy staff

**This is genuinely and substantially better than any prior era**, and is one of the few
unambiguous improvements in the business.

### 6.2 The historical reality (pre-2007)
- Work hurt, or lose your spot
- Painkillers freely available and widely used
- Concussions not recognised as injuries; "getting your bell rung" was a joke
- No paid time off; injured meant unpaid
- No guaranteed medical coverage

The consequences were catastrophic. **The mortality rate among wrestlers who worked in the
1980s–1990s is dramatically elevated** relative to the general population and to other
athletes: a long list of deaths before age 50 from heart failure, overdose, and suicide.
The causes were compounding — anabolic steroid use, painkiller dependency to work through
injury, recreational drug use in a road culture, no medical oversight, and cardiac strain
from size gain.

**The 2007 Benoit tragedy** was the inflection point that forced institutional change. The
wellness policy predates it (2006, following Eddie Guerrero's death in 2005) but was
substantially strengthened after.

### 6.3 Writing injuries into stories
Standard and useful: an injury becomes an angle (a beatdown that "caused" it), which
- Explains the absence in kayfabe
- Builds heat for a returning revenge feud
- Gives the injured performer a story waiting when they come back

The risk: the audience's inability to distinguish real from worked injuries produces
cynicism, and a real career-threatening injury can be met with "is this a work?"

## 7. The return

Returning from a long absence is one of wrestling's most reliable heat generators, and one
of its most mishandled moments.

**What works:**
- Return at a big show, unannounced, into an existing story
- Have something to do immediately
- The revenge motive against whoever "caused" it
- Acknowledge the absence rather than ignoring it

**What fails:**
- Returning on a random Tuesday with no story
- Returning and losing immediately
- Returning to the exact position they left, as if nothing happened
- Over-teasing the return until the surprise is gone

**The absence bonus:** a returning performer carries a temporary heat premium of roughly
3–8 weeks. What you do with those weeks determines whether the return was worth anything.
See [17-heat-and-getting-over.md](17-heat-and-getting-over.md) §7.

## 8. Attrition beyond injury

Careers also end from:
- **Burnout** — the schedule, the travel, the constant evaluation
- **Family** — the single most-cited voluntary reason
- **Financial** — needing an income that isn't wrestling
- **Loss of opportunity** — no promotion will book you at a level worth the physical cost
- **Better offers elsewhere** — acting, media, business
- **Mental health** — increasingly discussed and increasingly acknowledged

## Sim implications

- **Injury should be probabilistic and driven by real factors**: match volume, style,
  age, injury history, opponent safety, conditioning, and ring quality. The multipliers in
  §3.1 are directly usable.
- **Injury history as a permanent, compounding attribute** is the most important detail —
  a performer with a prior neck injury should be permanently more fragile.
- **The aging curve with separate tracks** for athleticism (declines from ~28), psychology
  (rises to ~40), and character/connection (rises with tenure) reproduces the real career
  arc and makes veteran management interesting. The existing `Wrestler` model already has
  the component stats to do this — it needs an age and a per-stat aging curve.
- **Drawing power peaking after athletic peak** creates the authentic tension: your most
  valuable performers are your most fragile.
- **Style-adjusted longevity** makes match-type choices carry a long-term cost, which turns
  "book the spotfest" into a real trade rather than a free rating.
- **Era-dependent medical policy** changes injury outcomes, career length, and mortality —
  a strong historical-mode mechanic.
- **The absence/return heat bonus** is a clean, implementable mechanic that also gives
  injuries a creative upside, exactly as in reality.
