# 19 — Characters and Promos

*Gimmicks, mic work, alignment, turns, and why character is the actual product.*

---

## 1. The thesis

**Wrestling sells characters. Matches are where characters pay off.**

Evidence: the highest-drawing performers in history are, with very few exceptions, not the
best wrestlers of their era. They are the ones whose characters people wanted to see. The
audience buys a ticket to see *a person*, not *a match*.

This is not a slight on in-ring work — great matches are how a character earns credibility
and how the audience's investment is rewarded. But the causal order runs
**character → investment → match matters**, not the reverse.

---

## 2. What a gimmick is

A **gimmick** is the character a performer plays. It comprises:

| Element | Function |
|---|---|
| **The name** | Memorability, connotation |
| **The look** | Gear, hair, physique, colours. Read instantly, before anything else. |
| **The entrance** | Music, lighting, movement. Often the single most-remembered element. |
| **The motivation** | What they want. The most-neglected element and the most important. |
| **The attitude** | How they carry themselves |
| **The catchphrase** | A participation hook |
| **The moveset** | Offence that fits the character |
| **The finisher** | Their signature moment |
| **The alignment** | Face, heel, tweener |

### 2.1 The types of gimmick
| Type | Example archetype | Durability |
|---|---|---|
| **The amplified self** | The performer, louder | **Highest.** Sustainable indefinitely because it's real. |
| **The occupation** | Wrestling plumber, wrestling dentist | Low. Dates badly, hard to take seriously. |
| **The monster** | Unstoppable, silent, dominant | Medium. Requires protection; collapses when beaten too often. |
| **The anti-hero** | Does what he wants, cheats, and is cheered | **Very high.** The dominant modern archetype since 1997. |
| **The authority-defier** | Fights the system | High, but requires an authority figure to fight |
| **The aristocrat/snob** | Looks down on the audience | High. Ancient and reliable heel formula. |
| **The foreigner** | Nationalist heat | Historically very effective, ethically fraught, largely retired |
| **The technician** | Sport-first, no gimmick | Medium. Requires elite work; ceiling is respect rather than love. |
| **The showman** | Charisma-forward entertainer | High |
| **The tragic/sympathetic** | Underdog, wronged, struggling | High, but must eventually win something |
| **The cult leader / supernatural** | Mystical, ominous | Medium-high; extremely powerful when it lands, ridiculous when it doesn't |
| **The comedy act** | Funny | Medium; a one-way door (see §5.3) |

### 2.2 The rule of gimmick success
**A gimmick works when it is 80% the performer and 20% amplification.** The failures are
almost always characters imposed on someone who has no personal connection to them —
which is why the same performer can flop under one name and become a phenomenon under
another with essentially the same body and skills.

### 2.3 Why gimmicks fail
- The performer doesn't believe in it (visible instantly)
- It has no motivation, only an aesthetic
- It's a joke the audience gets in five seconds and then has nothing else
- It requires the audience to accept something they won't (magic, an implausible premise)
- It has no path to a serious match
- It's derivative of something the audience already has
- **It was assigned rather than developed**

## 3. Promos

### 3.1 What a promo is for
1. Explain why the match matters
2. Reveal character
3. Generate emotion — anger, sympathy, excitement
4. Sell the ticket

A promo that does none of these is filler, and audiences identify filler within about
fifteen seconds.

### 3.2 The types
| Type | Setting | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Ring promo** | In the ring, live crowd | The highest-impact; direct address, crowd interaction |
| **Backstage interview** | With an interviewer | Efficient, low-energy, good for information |
| **Face-to-face confrontation** | Both parties | The most heat-generating format |
| **Vignette / pre-taped** | Produced package | Total control; used for debuts and character introduction |
| **Commentary-desk promo** | Sitting in | Intimate, conspiratorial |
| **The in-ring interruption** | Someone comes out | The workhorse structure of TV wrestling |

### 3.3 What makes a great promo
- **Conviction.** The audience believes the performer means it. Outweighs everything else.
- **Specificity.** "I'm going to beat you" is nothing. "I'm going to do it in your hometown
  in front of your father" is a promo.
- **Economy.** The best promos are shorter than people remember.
- **A hook the audience can repeat.**
- **Escalation** within the promo itself
- **Truth in it.** The best promos contain something real.
- **The crowd used as a scene partner** — pausing for reactions, responding to chants,
  letting them into it

### 3.4 What kills a promo
- Reciting a script
- Explaining the storyline the audience just watched
- Threatening violence generically
- Talking too long
- Talking *at* the camera when there's a live crowd, or vice versa
- Laughing at your own material
- Comedy that undercuts the threat you just established
- **Being interrupted before you've said anything** (a real structural problem in modern TV,
  where every promo exists to set up an interruption)

### 3.5 The scripting question
See [05-creative-process.md](05-creative-process.md) §5. The short version: full scripting
raises the floor and lowers the ceiling. Every legendary promo in wrestling history was
either unscripted or written by the performer.

## 4. Alignment: face, heel, tweener

### 4.1 The classic definitions
- **Face (babyface):** the one you're supposed to cheer. Fights fair, respects the audience,
  perseveres.
- **Heel:** the one you're supposed to boo. Cheats, disrespects, takes shortcuts.
- **Tweener:** neither, or both. Does what they want.

### 4.2 What actually determines alignment
**Not behaviour — the audience's reaction.** A performer is a heel if the audience boos
them, whatever they do. This is why the 1997+ era exists: audiences began cheering
characters who behaved like heels, because those characters were *cool*, and the promotions
had to adapt.

### 4.3 The modern reality
Since roughly 1997, strict alignment has eroded:
- Audiences cheer whoever they find compelling
- Anti-heroes are the dominant babyface archetype
- The "cool heel" is common and structurally problematic (see
  [16-crowd-psychology.md](16-crowd-psychology.md) §2.2)
- Some performers are cheered in one city and booed in another

**But the underlying mechanism still requires opposition.** A wrestling match needs someone
the audience wants to see win and someone they want to see lose. When neither exists, the
match has no emotional engine regardless of quality. This is why "everyone is a tweener" is
a failure state, not sophistication.

### 4.4 The heel's actual job
Widely misunderstood. A heel's job is **not** to be hated for its own sake. It is to:
1. Make the babyface's eventual victory feel earned
2. Generate the desire for that victory
3. Be credible enough that the outcome is in doubt

**A heel who cannot win is worthless.** The heat comes from the threat, not the sneering.
This is why "cowardly heel who always loses" is a dead end and why the best heels are the
ones who look genuinely capable of winning.

### 4.5 Heat generation methods, ranked by durability
1. **Being genuinely good and rubbing it in** — durable, sophisticated
2. **Taking something from a babyface the audience loves** — very strong
3. **Cheating to win** — the classic, reliable, sustainable
4. **Insulting the audience/the town** — cheap, effective, instantly renewable, shallow
5. **Attacking a beloved veteran or a non-wrestler** — strong, one-shot
6. **Being physically repellent or disturbing** — works, ages badly
7. **Being annoying** — produces go-away heat, not real heat. **A trap.**

The distinction between (3) and (7) is the difference between a heel who draws money and a
heel the audience wants off television.

## 5. Turns

The single largest one-shot heat generator in wrestling.

### 5.1 Why they work
A turn resets a character completely: new motivation, new opponents, new merchandise, new
promos, restored freshness. It is the cheapest full character reboot available.

### 5.2 The rules of a good turn
1. **It must be a surprise but make sense in retrospect.** The best turns have seeds planted
   weeks or months in advance.
2. **It must be motivated.** "Why?" needs an answer the audience accepts.
3. **It must be decisive.** A half-turn confuses.
4. **It must be at a moment of maximum consequence** — betray someone at the point where the
   betrayal costs the most.
5. **The turn's aftermath must be planned.** A turn with no follow-up wastes the biggest
   asset in booking.

### 5.3 The asymmetry of turns
- **Heel turns are easier and more reliably effective.** The audience enjoys betrayal.
- **Face turns are harder** — the audience must forgive, and forgiveness requires either a
  greater villain to unite against, or a genuine act of sympathy.
- **Comedy → serious is the hardest transition in wrestling.** Once the audience has laughed
  at you, threat is very difficult to re-establish. It has been done (Foley's Mankind, Kane's
  various phases) but it requires a genuine reinvention, not a tone shift.

### 5.4 Turn frequency
The most-violated rule: **turns should be rare.** A performer who turns every year has no
alignment, therefore no baseline, therefore no turn is meaningful. Roughly **once every
2–4 years per performer** is the sustainable rate. WCW's late-90s practice of turning
everyone repeatedly is the canonical demonstration of what happens when alignment stops
meaning anything.

## 6. Character evolution

Characters must change or die, but change badly and you lose the audience.

### 6.1 The good evolution
- **Same person, new circumstance** — the character grows without being replaced
- **A revealed dimension** — something we didn't know, consistent with what we did
- **Aging into a new role** — the young hothead becomes the veteran leader
- **Consequence** — an event genuinely changes them

### 6.2 The bad evolution
- **The reset** — the character's history is ignored and they start over as someone else
- **The unearned upgrade** — suddenly serious, suddenly dominant, with no cause
- **The flip-flop** — changing back and forth as booking convenience requires
- **The writer's-room reinvention** — a new writer arrives and rewrites the character to
  their taste. The most common cause of character damage at Tier 0.

### 6.3 The longest-running characters
The characters that lasted decades (Undertaker, Sting, Ric Flair, Rey Mysterio) all worked
the same way: a **fixed core** with **periodic surface reinvention**. The Undertaker's
essential premise never changed across thirty years; the presentation changed radically
four or five times. That is the template.

## 7. Managers, mouthpieces, and the delegation of talk

A manager solves the most common talent problem in wrestling: a performer with presence and
ability who cannot cut a promo.

**Why the role works:**
- The manager generates heat the wrestler doesn't have to
- The wrestler stays mysterious/monstrous by not speaking
- The manager is a physical target — the babyface wants to get their hands on them
- One manager can support several acts

**Why it declined:** the shift to fully scripted promos for everyone, plus a belief that a
top star must be able to speak for themselves. Periodically rediscovered — Paul Heyman's
work with Brock Lesnar and later Roman Reigns is the modern demonstration that the role
still works at the very top.

## 8. Names, music, and presentation

Small things with disproportionate effect:

- **The name.** Must be memorable, pronounceable, and not sound like everyone else's.
  Two-syllable first names chant well. A name the crowd can chant is worth real money.
- **The music.** The single most efficient character-establishing tool that exists. The
  audience learns to react to the first two seconds. Changing a successful theme is one of
  the most damaging things a promotion can do to an over performer.
- **Colours and silhouette.** The audience must be able to identify a performer at a glance
  in a wide shot.
- **The entrance.** A great entrance is worth 20–30% of a reaction. It is also the most
  reliably reusable asset — a character who has nothing else can still have an entrance
  that pops a building.

## Sim implications

- The existing `Gimmick` model (name, type, tone, alignment, fan-group appeal, freshness) is
  a strong foundation. The additions worth making:
  - **Motivation** as a field — because a gimmick without one is the identified failure mode
  - **A participation hook** flag (catchphrase/chant) that grants a crowd bonus scaling
    with audience tenure
  - **Fit** — how well the gimmick matches the performer's real attributes. The "80% the
    performer" rule suggests a compatibility score between gimmick type and the wrestler's
    charisma/tone/style, which would gate how much of the gimmick's potential is realised.
- **Alignment should be derived from audience reaction, not declared.** The sim already
  computes crowd disposition from popularity and fan-group appeal rather than alignment —
  which is exactly right. The next step is letting the *effective* alignment drift away from
  the *booked* one, and making that drift visible and consequential.
- **Turns as a high-value, cooldown-gated action:** large one-shot heat, freshness reset,
  with diminishing returns if used more often than every 2–4 years per performer.
- **The comedy one-way door** — a flag that makes a character much harder to book as a
  serious threat afterwards — is a small mechanic with real strategic weight.
- **Promo quality should have two inputs**, scripted delivery and improvisation, with the
  promotion's scripting policy determining which one is used. A promotion that scripts
  everything should get consistent mid-range results; one that doesn't should get high
  variance. That single choice reproduces a real industry debate as a game decision.
- **Music/entrance as a separate presentation value** that contributes to crowd energy at
  the start of a match, and which the player can invest in.
