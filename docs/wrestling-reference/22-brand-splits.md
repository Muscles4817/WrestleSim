# 22 — Brand Splits

*Why promotions divide themselves into separate rosters, what makes it work, what makes it
fail, and how it is sustained.*

This answers the user questions: **"What motivates brand splits? What sustains brand
splits? What makes them work? What makes them fail?"**

---

## 1. What a brand split is

A **brand split** divides one promotion into two or more semi-autonomous units, each with:
- Its own roster (exclusive, or mostly so)
- Its own television show
- Its own championships
- Often its own creative team, general manager/authority figure, and identity

The performers all work for the same company; the *fiction* is that the brands are separate
competing entities.

### 1.1 The canonical examples
| Split | Period | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **WWE Raw / SmackDown (first)** | 2002–2011 | Ran nine years; gradually eroded; formally abandoned |
| **WWE Raw / SmackDown (second)** | 2016–~2023 | Reinstated, then gradually eroded again |
| **WWE + NXT as a third brand** | 2019–2021 ("Wednesday Night War") | NXT positioned as a competing brand, then repositioned as developmental |
| **WCW's nWo** | 1996–1999 | Not a formal split but functionally one — a separate faction with its own identity that eventually got its own show (nWo Souled Out as a "separate" PPV) |
| **AEW / ROH** | 2022– | A genuine two-promotion structure under one owner |
| **NJPW's units** | ongoing | Not a brand split but a faction system that performs a similar organising function |
| **Stardom / NJPW under Bushiroad** | ongoing | Separate promotions, shared ownership, occasional crossover |

## 2. What motivates a brand split

### 2.1 The roster is bigger than the television
**The primary and most legitimate motive.** A promotion with 120 contracted performers and
one weekly show can feature perhaps 25 of them. Splitting the roster across two shows
doubles the meaningful screen time available per performer.

This was the explicit reason for WWE's 2002 split: the WCW and ECW acquisitions had left the
company with an enormous roster and nowhere to put it.

### 2.2 Two television properties need two identities
If a network buys a second show, it wants something distinct — not a B-show of the first.
A brand split gives each show its own stars, its own titles, and its own reason to exist.
**This is often the real driver**: the split is a media-rights requirement dressed as a
creative decision.

### 2.3 More main-event slots
Two brands = two world champions = two main-event scenes = roughly twice as many performers
at the top of a card. For a promotion with a deep roster and a star-creation problem, this
is genuinely valuable.

### 2.4 Twice the titles, twice the merchandise, twice the tours
Two brands can run two simultaneous touring units, doubling live-event capacity without
doubling the schedule burden on any individual.

### 2.5 Internal competition
The theory: two creative teams competing produces better television. In practice this is
the least-supported motive — it usually produces territorialism and resource fights rather
than quality.

### 2.6 Protecting talent from being buried by the top of the card
On a unified roster, the top 5 absorb all the oxygen. A split gives the 6th–15th best
performers a card where they *are* the top.

## 3. What makes a brand split work

### 3.1 Genuine exclusivity
**The single most important factor.** If performers appear on both shows freely, there is no
split — there is one roster with two timeslots, and the audience knows it immediately.

Exclusivity must be enforced:
- Roster members appear only on their brand
- Titles are defended only on their brand
- Crossover requires a story reason and an on-screen acknowledgement

The moment exclusivity becomes optional, the split has functionally ended, whatever the
company still says on television.

### 3.2 Distinct identities
The two brands must **feel different**, not just have different names:
- Different aesthetic (set, colours, music, graphics)
- Different tone (one sports-focused, one entertainment-focused; one grittier, one glossier)
- Different match styles emphasised
- Different commentary teams
- Different pacing

The 2002–2005 WWE split partially achieved this (SmackDown's "Six" era developed a distinct
in-ring identity around Angle, Benoit, Guerrero, Mysterio, Edge). When brands look and feel
identical, the split adds administrative complexity and no audience-facing value.

### 3.3 Equal investment
The split fails immediately if one brand is visibly the A-show and one is the B-show.
Symptoms of unequal investment:
- The bigger stars all on one brand
- The prestigious title on one brand
- One brand gets the tentpole main events
- One brand's champions appear on the other's show but not vice versa

**The B-show problem is the most common cause of brand-split failure**, and it is
self-reinforcing: talent doesn't want to be on the B-show, so the B-show's roster requests
moves, so it gets worse.

### 3.4 Real stakes for the brand itself
The split needs a reason to matter in the fiction. Devices that work:
- **Inter-brand competition events** (Survivor Series as a brand-vs-brand show is the
  strongest version — it gives every match a stake beyond itself)
- **The draft** — an annual event where brands compete for talent, which is genuinely
  compelling television and reinforces that brand membership matters
- **General managers with rivalries** — the brands' representatives competing
- **Brand pride as a character motivation**

Without stakes, "which show is this person on" is scheduling trivia.

### 3.5 Separate creative with a shared destination
Each brand needs its own creative attention or one will be an afterthought. But there must
be someone above both ensuring the company-level story (the tentpole main event, the
top-star trajectory) is coherent.

### 3.6 Enough depth
**The hard requirement:** each brand needs roughly **25–40 usable performers**, including
4–6 credible main-eventers, 6–10 upper card, a functioning tag division, and a functioning
women's division. A promotion without 60–80 usable performers cannot split without both
brands looking thin.

## 4. What makes a brand split fail

### 4.1 Crossover erosion (the universal failure mode)
The pattern is remarkably consistent:

```
  Month 1–6      Strict exclusivity. The split feels real.
  Month 6–12     A special "crossover" appearance for a big angle. Justified.
  Year 1–2       Crossovers become common for big shows.
  Year 2–3       Top stars appear on both shows regularly "because they're stars".
  Year 3+        The split exists only in graphics. Titles are defended anywhere.
                 Announcers stop mentioning brands.
  Eventually     Officially abandoned, or quietly ignored until someone announces
                 it's over.
```

**Why it happens:** every individual crossover is defensible. The biggest star drawing the
biggest rating on the show that needs it most is always the locally correct decision. The
aggregate of locally correct decisions destroys the structure. This is a pure tragedy-of-
the-commons dynamic and it has killed every WWE brand split.

### 4.2 The B-show spiral
See §3.3. Once one brand is visibly secondary:
- Its ratings drop, justifying less investment
- Talent asks to move
- Its titles are worth less
- Its main event isn't a main event
- The audience treats it as optional

### 4.3 Diluted championships
Two world titles means neither is *the* world title. The audience will decide which one is
real, and it usually isn't the one management wants. Every brand split has to answer "who
is the actual champion of this company?" and the honest answer is always "one of them".

### 4.4 Insufficient depth
Splitting a roster that isn't deep enough produces two thin shows instead of one strong one.
This is the most common failure at Tier 1 and below.

### 4.5 Creative-resource dilution
Two shows need twice the ideas. If the creative capacity doesn't double, both shows get
worse. See [05-creative-process.md](05-creative-process.md) §10.

### 4.6 Fan fatigue with the concept
Audiences have limited patience for administrative fiction. "Why can't he wrestle her, they
work for the same company?" is a question with no satisfying answer, and every year the
split runs makes it louder.

### 4.7 It solves a problem that goes away
If the split existed because the roster was too big, and the roster shrinks (releases,
departures, injuries), the split's rationale evaporates but the structure persists.

## 5. What sustains a brand split

The things that keep it alive, in order of importance:

1. **Enforced exclusivity, with someone empowered to say no** to crossovers. Without this,
   nothing else matters.
2. **The draft as an annual event** — it refreshes rosters, creates a genuine television
   moment, resets stale pairings, and re-establishes that brand membership is real.
3. **Inter-brand events** (Survivor Series-style) that give the split a payoff.
4. **Distinct identities** maintained deliberately.
5. **Separate championships with separate lineages**, protected from each other.
6. **Equal booking of the tentpole shows** — both brands' main events matter at
   WrestleMania.
7. **Talent movement being a story**, not an administrative note. A trade should be an event.
8. **The company treating both shows as its show**, visible in production budget and
   executive attention.

### 5.1 The draft as a mechanism
Worth separate emphasis because it is the best single tool for sustaining a split:
- It is compelling television in itself
- It refreshes every roster and every possible match-up simultaneously
- It resolves stale feuds and creates new ones automatically
- It lets the promotion correct imbalances between brands
- It gives every performer a moment of uncertainty and a new context

**Annual cadence is right.** More often and it destabilises; less often and rosters stagnate.

## 6. When a brand split is the wrong answer

- **Roster under 60 usable performers** — split two thin shows out of one thin show
- **Creative team already stretched**
- **One show has substantially higher ratings/rights value** and always will — the
  structural inequality can't be fixed
- **The real problem is that the top of the card is stale** — a split doesn't fix that, it
  just gives you two stale main events
- **When the company can't resist crossovers** — an organisation without the discipline to
  enforce it shouldn't attempt it

**The alternative to a brand split** for a too-large roster is usually better: fewer
contracts, a real developmental system, more house-show touring to give people reps, or a
secondary promotion with a genuinely different audience (the AEW/ROH structure).

## 7. Related structures that aren't brand splits

### 7.1 The faction system (NJPW)
NJPW organises its roster into **units** (Bullet Club, CHAOS, Los Ingobernables de Japón,
United Empire, etc.) rather than brands. This achieves several of the same goals:
- Distributes the roster into legible groups
- Creates automatic alliances and rivalries
- Gives mid-card performers an identity beyond themselves
- Generates multi-man matches with built-in stakes

**Advantages over a brand split:** no exclusivity enforcement problem, no title dilution, no
B-show risk, and it costs no additional television.
**Disadvantage:** it doesn't create additional main-event slots or additional TV inventory.

### 7.2 The sister promotion
Two separate companies under one owner (AEW/ROH; Bushiroad's NJPW/Stardom; CyberAgent's
DDT/NOAH). Cleaner than a brand split because the separation is real: different brands,
different audiences, different venues, occasional crossover as a genuine event.

**This is arguably the superior structure** for the same goals, because the separation
doesn't require the audience to accept a fiction.

### 7.3 The developmental brand
NXT-style: a genuinely subordinate brand whose purpose is development, presented honestly as
such. Works well because the hierarchy is admitted rather than denied. Becomes problematic
when it is positioned as a third equal brand, because then it's a brand split with all the
same problems.

---

## 8. The honest assessment

Brand splits are **an administrative solution to a creative problem**, and their track
record is mixed. They reliably deliver:
- More screen time per performer ✓
- More main-event slots ✓
- More titles and tours ✓

And they reliably fail to deliver:
- Distinct identities that survive past year two ✗
- Equal prestige between brands ✗
- Enforcement of the exclusivity that makes them meaningful ✗

**The determining variable is organisational discipline**, not creative quality. A promotion
willing to keep its biggest star off half its television for the sake of a structure can
sustain a split. Almost no promotion is willing to do that for long, because the short-term
cost is visible and the long-term benefit is not.

## Sim implications

- **Brands as first-class entities** with their own roster, shows, titles, identity, and
  audience is a substantial but high-value feature. It creates a genuinely different
  management layer.
- **The crossover temptation as an explicit mechanic** is the best part: let the player put
  their biggest star on the other brand's show for an immediate ratings boost, and have it
  permanently reduce a "split integrity" value that governs whether the brands feel distinct.
  That single loop reproduces the entire real-world dynamic.
- **Split integrity** should modify: each brand's ability to generate its own stars, the
  prestige of its titles, and audience acceptance of brand-based stakes.
- **The B-show spiral** should be an emergent failure: talent requesting moves, ratings
  divergence, and title prestige drift, all driven by unequal booking.
- **The draft as an annual event** with real mechanical effects (roster reshuffle, freshness
  reset on all pairings, feud terminations) would be one of the most satisfying recurring
  events in the game.
- **Depth requirements** (60–80 usable performers, 25–40 per brand) should gate the option,
  with visible warnings when a promotion splits too early.
- **Faction systems as an alternative** to a split — cheaper, no exclusivity requirement,
  different benefits — gives the player a real strategic choice between two ways to organise
  a large roster.
