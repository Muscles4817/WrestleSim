# 02 — Anatomy of a Promotion

*What a wrestling company physically is: who owns it, what departments exist, how authority
actually flows, and how that changes with size.*

---

## 1. Ownership models

Ownership determines the decision-making structure more than any other single factor.

### 1.1 Owner-operator (the historical default)
One person owns the company and books it, or owns it and hires the booker but retains final
say. Vince McMahon (WWF/WWE 1982–2022), Verne Gagne (AWA), Fritz Von Erich (WCCW), Jerry
Jarrett (Memphis), Paul Heyman (ECW), Tony Khan (AEW).

**Properties:**
- Decisions are fast. No committee, no approval chain, no quarterly review.
- Creative coherence is high *if the owner is good* and catastrophic if not, because there
  is no mechanism to overrule them.
- Succession is the existential risk. Almost every territory died within five years of its
  founder's decline, death, or disengagement.
- The owner's personal taste becomes the company's aesthetic. This is a feature for a
  decade and a liability for the decade after.

**The structural pathology:** owner-operators have no feedback loop that can override them.
Ratings drop, business drops, and the owner's explanation ("the talent isn't getting it
done", "the audience is wrong") is unfalsifiable from inside the building. Every famous
collapse in wrestling history has this shape.

### 1.2 Publicly traded (WWE 1999–2023; TKO 2023–)
**Properties:**
- Quarterly earnings pressure. Decisions get optimised for reportable metrics —
  subscriptions, then "engagement", then rights fees.
- Real financial disclosure. This is why WWE's numbers are known and everyone else's aren't.
- Legal and compliance constraints on content (the 2000s "PG era" was substantially driven
  by advertiser and shareholder pressure, not creative preference).
- Talent costs become a line item that analysts ask about, which creates a structural bias
  toward roster cuts in Q1 and Q3 regardless of creative need.
- Board oversight exists but historically did not constrain a controlling shareholder; WWE
  had a dual-class structure that made McMahon effectively unremovable until the 2022–2024
  sequence.

### 1.3 Private, funded by a wealthy backer (AEW; many failed challengers)
- Runway is a function of the backer's patience and net worth, not profitability.
- Freed from quarterly reporting, which permits genuinely long creative arcs.
- But also freed from *any* external discipline, which permits sustained overspending on
  a roster larger than the television can feature. This is AEW's characteristic problem and
  was WCW's fatal one.
- The backer's other business interests matter enormously (Khan family: Jacksonville
  Jaguars, Fulham FC — provides venue relationships, corporate infrastructure, and a
  credible balance sheet).

### 1.4 Corporate subsidiary (NJPW under Bushiroad; NOAH and DDT under CyberAgent; Impact/TNA under Anthem; WCW under Turner/Time Warner)
- The promotion is an asset in a portfolio, judged against the portfolio's goals.
- **Best case:** stable capital, professional management, patient timelines. Bushiroad's
  NJPW ownership has been broadly this.
- **Worst case:** the parent's strategy changes and wrestling is orphaned. WCW died not
  because it was unprofitable at its peak but because the AOL–Time Warner merger produced
  executives who did not want wrestling on the network at any price. **A promotion inside a
  media conglomerate can be killed by a decision that has nothing to do with wrestling.**
- Reporting lines get long. A booker who has to get an angle approved by a network standards
  department three levels up will book blander television.

### 1.5 Cooperative / family / commission-governed (CMLL, and lucha generally)
- CMLL is family-held across four generations, and Mexican lucha operates partly under
  athletic-commission authority: masks and hair matches have legal weight, and identities
  are registered.
- Produces extreme continuity — CMLL has run Friday nights at Arena México since 1933 —
  and extreme conservatism.

## 2. The org chart

The functions below exist at every tier. At Tier 0 each is a department; at Tier 4 one
person does all of them.

```
                              OWNERSHIP / BOARD
                                     │
                          ┌──────────┴──────────┐
                          │    CEO / PRESIDENT   │
                          └──────────┬──────────┘
        ┌──────────────┬─────────────┼─────────────┬──────────────┐
        │              │             │             │              │
    CREATIVE      TALENT REL.    OPERATIONS    BUSINESS       LEGAL/HR
        │              │             │             │              │
  ┌─────┴─────┐   ┌────┴────┐   ┌────┴────┐  ┌─────┴─────┐        │
  Head of      Contracts     Live Events   Media Rights          Compliance
  Creative     Scouting      Production    Sponsorship           Wellness
  Writers      Developmental Travel/Logistics Merchandise        Contracts
  Producers    Wellness      Ring Crew     Ticketing             Litigation
  (Agents)     Discipline    Security      Digital/Social
  Talent Dev   Relations     Referees      Consumer Products
```

### 2.1 Creative
The department that decides what happens on screen.
- **Head of Creative / Booker** — final creative authority below the owner
- **Writers** — at Tier 0 only; typically 15–25 people, split by show (Raw team, SmackDown
  team), often from television/comedy backgrounds rather than wrestling
- **Producers ("agents", "road agents")** — former wrestlers who lay out matches with the
  performers and translate creative's intent into an actual match
- **Talent development / coaches** — in the training centre

### 2.2 Talent Relations
- Contracts, renewals, releases
- Scouting and recruitment
- The interface between the roster and management; the person a wrestler calls when there's
  a problem
- **Structural tension:** this department is simultaneously the talent's advocate and the
  company's negotiator. Every wrestler knows this. It is why the role is so often disliked
  and why the person holding it (Johnny Ace/John Laurinaitis, Jim Ross before him, Mark
  Carrano, Canyon Ceman) becomes a lightning rod.

### 2.3 Operations / Live Events
- Booking buildings (18–24 months in advance at Tier 0)
- Routing tours, travel, hotels
- Ring crew, production trucks, lighting, pyro, staging
- Security, medical staff, local promoter liaison
- **The unglamorous department that determines whether the company can physically exist.**
  A promotion that cannot reliably put a ring in a building on a Tuesday is not a promotion.

### 2.4 Television Production
Often the largest headcount at Tier 0/1. Directors, producers, camera, audio, graphics,
editors, commentary, the truck. Wrestling television is a **live multi-camera broadcast
produced on a two-hour turnaround in a different city every week**, which is genuinely one
of the harder production jobs in television.

### 2.5 Business / Commercial
- **Media rights** — the largest revenue line; renegotiated on 3–5 year cycles
- **Sponsorship** — arena signage, segment sponsorships, integrations
- **Merchandise and licensing** — t-shirts, toys (Mattel/Jakks), video games (2K), collectibles
- **Ticketing** — often outsourced to the venue's system; dynamic pricing since the 2010s
- **Digital/social** — YouTube, TikTok, short-form; at WWE this is a genuine revenue line
  (billions of annual views) and at every other promotion it is marketing

### 2.6 Legal / HR / Wellness
- Talent contracts (independent contractor status — see
  [11-contracts-and-talent-money.md](11-contracts-and-talent-money.md))
- Trademark portfolio (a major's IP is thousands of marks — names, logos, catchphrases)
- Wellness policy / drug testing (WWE's dates from 2006, post-Eddie Guerrero; strengthened
  post-Benoit 2007)
- Concussion protocol, medical clearance

## 3. How authority actually flows (versus the chart)

The org chart is not how decisions get made. The real structure at most promotions:

```
                          THE OWNER'S OPINION
                                  │
                    ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐
                    │                           │
          What the owner watched          Who spoke to the owner
          on the last show                 most recently
                    │                           │
                    └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                                  ▼
                          TODAY'S CREATIVE
```

This is only partly a joke. Documented features of real wrestling decision-making:

- **Recency dominance.** A single crowd reaction, a single bad match, or a single
  conversation on a plane routinely reverses months of planning. Vince McMahon rewriting
  Raw on the day of the show — sometimes during the show — was standard practice for two
  decades and is the most-cited source of writer burnout and talent uncertainty.
- **Proximity is power.** Whoever travels with, trains with, or has the ear of the decision
  maker has influence disproportionate to their title. This is the mechanical basis of
  "backstage politics" — see [14-locker-room.md](14-locker-room.md).
- **The veto is stronger than the pitch.** Most creative decisions are made by elimination:
  an idea survives because nobody senior killed it, not because it was chosen.
- **Nobody owns "no".** In a healthy organisation someone can say "this doesn't work" to
  the top. In most promotions, no one can, and the entire company watches a bad idea
  proceed knowing it is bad. Ask anyone who worked on a famously failed angle: the standard
  account is "everyone knew".

### 3.1 The healthy version
Promotions that have functioned well over long periods generally have:
- One person with **final say** (committee booking is reliably worse — see
  [04-booking-philosophy.md](04-booking-philosophy.md))
- At least one person who can **credibly tell that person no** and survive doing it
- A **feedback mechanism external to the owner's taste** — attendance in specific markets,
  merch sales by performer, crowd reaction data, ratings by quarter-hour
- **Written long-term plans** that survive contact with a bad Tuesday

Paul Heyman in ECW had (1) and (4) but nothing else and burned the company out in six
years. Vince McMahon in 1997–2000 had (1), and Jim Ross/Bruce Prichard/Pat Patterson gave
him a partial (2). Tony Khan has (1) and cultivates (4) heavily via metrics but has been
publicly criticised for lacking (2).

## 4. What a promotion looks like at each size

### Tier 0 (WWE-scale)
- 800–1,000 employees, 150–200 contracted performers
- A permanent headquarters (Stamford, CT — a purpose-built ~415,000 sq ft campus opened
  2022), a training facility (Performance Center, Orlando), and a media production
  operation that produces more first-run content than most cable networks
- Multiple simultaneous touring units
- Departments with department heads who have their own staffs
- **The performer's experience:** you have a producer assigned to your segment, a writer
  assigned to your character, a contract with a downside guarantee, a travel schedule
  handed to you, and almost no direct access to the person who decides your fate

### Tier 1 (AEW-scale)
- 200–400 employees including production
- Owner is directly reachable by most of the roster — a defining cultural difference
- One or two touring units
- Departments exist but are thin; individuals wear multiple hats
- **The performer's experience:** more creative freedom, more direct access, less structure,
  and a real chance of being forgotten on a 120-person roster with 4 hours of TV

### Tier 2/3
- 5–40 full-time staff
- The owner books, negotiates, and often drives the truck
- Production is contracted per-show
- **The performer's experience:** you are a freelancer; you get told the finish an hour
  before; you're responsible for your own travel and gear

### Tier 4
- The promoter, a spouse or partner doing the door and merch, a ring crew of the younger
  wrestlers, one referee, one person on music
- Venue rented per date; ring stored in a trailer or a garage
- Payoffs in cash from the gate after expenses

## 5. Physical infrastructure

Things people forget a promotion needs to own or rent:

| Asset | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Rings** | $8k–$25k each new; a touring major owns 6–12 plus spares. Ring trucks and crews travel separately and arrive first. |
| **Staging/set** | Tier 0 entrance sets are custom-built per show cycle, cost $500k–several million for tentpoles, and require a fleet of trucks. |
| **Production truck** | Rented per-show or owned; a full HD multi-cam truck is a $2–10M asset. |
| **Titles** | Physical belts cost $5k–$40k+ each; multiple copies exist (a "TV belt" and spares). |
| **Training facility** | WWE's Performance Center; AEW has used partner facilities and a Nightmare Factory relationship. Tier 2 and below rely on independent schools. |
| **Warehouse/office** | Merchandise inventory, props, historic tape library. |
| **Tape library** | WWE's video library (WWF + WCW + ECW + AWA + territory footage) is a genuinely major asset — it underpins the Network/Peacock/Netflix content offer and decades of licensing. Buying competitors' libraries is a repeated WWE strategy. |

## 6. The calendar as an organising structure

A promotion is best understood as a **calendar with obligations attached**:

- **Contracted television dates** — non-negotiable; if you have a 52-week deal you produce
  52 weeks
- **Contracted building dates** — booked 12–24 months out, with deposits and cancellation
  penalties
- **Tentpole events** — the annual anchors around which everything is planned
- **Talent contract expirations** — a rolling set of deadlines that drives creative
- **Media rights expiry** — the single most important date in the company; everything in the
  12–18 months before it is arranged to make the product look maximally valuable

Everything creative is fitted into that skeleton. This is why "why did they do that?"
so often has a scheduling answer rather than a creative one — the pay-per-view was already
booked into that building, the contract expired, the network wanted a special.

## Sim implications

- Model a promotion as **{ownership model, tier, calendar obligations, department
  competencies, authority structure}**. Ownership model should gate which decisions the
  player can make unilaterally.
- **Departments as competency scores** (Production, Talent Relations, Live Events,
  Marketing, Medical) gives non-creative levers that matter — a promotion with bad Live
  Events loses money on tours no matter how good the booking is.
- The **authority-flow model** is a genuinely interesting mechanic: who can say no to the
  player, and what happens when nobody can. An owner-operator with no counterweight should
  drift, and the sim should let that drift happen and cost something.
- **Recency dominance** could be modelled as a temptation rather than a rule: the game
  surfaces last night's reactions prominently, and overreacting to them is a mistake the
  player can make.
- Infrastructure (rings, trucks, sets, a tape library) is real capex and gives lower-tier
  promotions concrete growth goals beyond "get more fans".
